# GLOBAL INSIGHTS SCRATCHPAD

The following are global insights about optimization approaches and their effectiveness:

## Successful Algorithmic Patterns
- **The current best, Demand-Weighted Surrogate QUBO (Generation 6), remains the strongest of the newly evaluated programs.** Its score of **0.81** and **14.16% mean best gap** outperform Generations 5, 7, 8, and 9 on combined score. Its gaps were **11.2–17.8%** across the four cases, with network10_s1 best at **11.2%**.
- **Demand-conditioned two-hop rewards remain a useful but modest pattern.** Generation 6 rewards only paths \(i \to j \to k\) whose endpoint demand \(d[i,k]\) is positive, scaling the reward by relay load. It uses 480 quadratic terms and attained a better score and best gap than the broader or congestion-oriented alternatives in this batch. This is consistent with earlier results favoring sparse, demand-supported path terms over indiscriminate interactions.
- **Direct-demand weighting is a robust foundation, but the added path terms do not consistently close the gap.** Generation 6 combines negative demand-normalized link costs with discounted two-hop rewards; its best gap is better than Generation 8’s **17.92%** and Generation 9’s **15.05%**. However, earlier evidence still shows that a linear baseline can match or slightly beat richer models on mean best gap, so the benefit of quadratic rewards is limited rather than established as universal.
- **Feasibility and diversity remain strong across the approaches.** Generation 6 produced **100% valid samples before repair** and **64 distinct topologies per case**, as did Generations 5, 7, 8, and 9. These shared strengths are not what distinguish their solution gaps.

## Ineffective Approaches
- **More elaborate endpoint, relay, congestion, or reciprocity adjustments did not improve on Generation 6 in this batch.** Generation 5’s endpoint/transit usefulness and weak-reciprocal-link penalty scored **0.80** with a **14.97%** mean best gap; Generation 7’s endpoint-pressure, relay-attractiveness, and congestion adjustments also scored **0.80**, with a **13.72%** gap. Both trails Generation 6’s **0.81** score, though Generation 7’s best-gap metric is slightly better.
- **Congestion penalties performed particularly poorly.** Generation 8 adds positive penalties for potentially congested two-hop paths and has the weakest new results: **0.77** score, **17.92% mean best gap**, and **44.56% mean median gap**. Its per-case median gaps of **40.6–48.5%** support the earlier finding that congestion and shared-link interaction heuristics can worsen quality.
- **Capacity-weighted relay rewards did not translate into competitive typical samples.** Generation 9 scores **0.79**, with a **15.05% mean best gap** and **40.52% median gap**, despite restricting terms to positive-demand paths with eligible relays. Its median performance is worse than Generation 6’s **36.29%**, reinforcing that relay weighting alone does not ensure consistently good samples.
- **A moderate quadratic-term count is not sufficient for quality.** Generation 5 used **848** terms but scored **0.80**; Generations 6–9 each used **480**, yet their scores ranged from **0.77 to 0.81**. As earlier comparisons also indicated, term count alone does not predict surrogate accuracy.

## Implementation Insights
- **Generation 6’s central implementation pattern is normalized direct demand plus sparse, path-supported rewards.** It scales each directed link’s negative linear cost by maximum demand, and adds a negative interaction only for \(i \to j \to k\) where \(d[i,k] > 0\). This avoids rewarding unsupported routes and yields 480 quadratic terms.
- **Its relay-load discount is explicit and bounded:** the path reward is discounted by \(1 + \text{relay load}\), with relay load normalized by the maximum node load. The implementation uses a fixed degree-penalty weight of **1.0**. Despite simpler load treatment than Generation 7’s endpoint-pressure and congestion adjustments, it has the best combined score in this batch.
- **The other implementations show the risks of adding heuristic complexity.** Generation 5 combines direct demand and transit usefulness with joint-link bonuses and a reciprocal-link penalty; Generation 7 adjusts for endpoint pressure, relay attractiveness, and congestion; Generation 8 adds positive congestion penalties. Their scores (**0.80, 0.80, and 0.77**) do not surpass Generation 6.
- **Fast construction and full validity are consistent implementation properties, not differentiators.** All five new programs built in under **0.01 seconds**, produced 64 distinct topologies per case, and reported 100% feasibility. Generation 6’s performance advantage therefore appears in its objective approximation, not runtime or feasibility.

## Performance Analysis
- **Generation 6 leads the new batch on combined score:** **0.81**, versus **0.80** for Generations 5 and 7, **0.79** for Generation 9, and **0.77** for Generation 8. Its mean best gap of **14.16%** is better than Generations 5, 8, and 9, but worse than Generation 7’s **13.72%**.
- **The best-gap and median-gap rankings differ.** Generation 7 has the lowest mean best gap in this batch (**13.72%**) but the highest median gap (**37.61%**) among Generations 5–7. Generation 6 has a **36.29%** median gap; it is better than Generations 5, 7, 8, and 9, though still far above its own best-solution gap.
- **Case difficulty varies by surrogate.** Generation 6’s largest gap is network10_s3 (**17.8%**) and smallest is s1 (**11.2%**). Generation 5 instead identifies s0 as worst, while Generation 7’s gaps range up to **18.2%** and Generation 9’s worst case is s2. Thus, the apparent hardest case is not fixed across implementations.
- **The current best does not supersede the earlier best-gap findings.** Previous results reported a **12.76%** mean best gap for Generation 0’s linear baseline and **12.84%** for Generation 1, both below Generation 6’s **14.16%**. Generation 6’s **0.81** combined score is strong, but the available results do not establish that its two-hop and relay-load terms improve best-gap quality over the earlier simpler approaches.

# META RECOMMENDATIONS

The following are actionable recommendations for the next program generations:

1. **Run a small sweep over the direct-link and path-reward balance.** Keep the current positive-demand two-hop paths and relay discount, but test direct weights scaled around the current value (for example, `-0.8`, `-0.65`) alongside path coefficients around `0.08`, `0.16`, and `0.20` instead of `0.12`. The linear baseline’s slightly better mean best gap suggests that reducing the path reward may preserve its benefits while restoring emphasis on direct demand.

2. **Compare relay-discount variants without changing the supported-path set.** Test the current discount, no discount, and a stronger discount such as `1 / (1 + node_load[j] / max_node_load)`. This isolates whether the load adjustment helps: the current model performs well, but Generation 9’s capacity-weighted relay rewards had weaker typical samples, so avoid adding more elaborate congestion or capacity penalties.

3. **Use robust demand normalization while preserving direct-demand and path terms.** Compare maximum normalization with normalization by the mean of positive off-diagonal demands, and with maximum normalization after clipping exceptionally large demands. This directly tests whether a single large demand suppresses useful signal elsewhere, while retaining the sparse, demand-supported interactions associated with the current program’s strong results.

4. **Add a targeted, modest relay-connectivity factor to supported paths.** For each `i → j → k` with positive `d[i,k]`, scale its existing reward by a normalized measure of demand into `j` from `i` and out of `j` to `k`; keep the factor bounded and test it against the current load-only discount. This explores whether path-specific relay usefulness improves on total node load without introducing the broader interactions or congestion penalties that underperformed.

5. **Test a direct-demand-only ablation against the current surrogate, especially on the hardest case.** Set the quadratic coefficient to zero while leaving the normalized linear terms and penalty unchanged, then compare per-case best and median gaps with the current model. Earlier linear baselines achieved lower mean best gaps, and the current model’s largest gap is on `network10_s3`, so this is a focused check of whether two-hop rewards help there or dilute direct-link signal.
