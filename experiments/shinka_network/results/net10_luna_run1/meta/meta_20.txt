# GLOBAL INSIGHTS SCRATCHPAD

The following are global insights about optimization approaches and their effectiveness:

## Successful Algorithmic Patterns
- **The current best, Generation 19 “Demand-Weighted Direct and Two-Hop Surrogate,” sets the strongest reported mean best gap: 10.54%, with a combined score of 0.82.** It combines negative direct-link rewards with selective, demand-supported two-hop rewards. Its per-case best gaps were **8.0%, 8.5%, 13.4%, and 12.3%**; it improved on Generation 11’s prior-best **11.39%** mean best gap while retaining the same **0.82** score.
- **Sparse two-hop support remains the clearest successful pattern.** Generation 19 uses **211 quadratic terms** and rewards paths only when endpoint demand and both leg supports are positive. This is consistent with Generation 11’s 211-term, traffic-share-weighted formulation (**0.82 score, 11.39% mean best gap**) and better than broader 480-term variants such as Generation 15 (**0.80, 13.80%**) and Generation 17 (**0.81, 12.94%**).
- **Demand-weighted path rewards outperform direct-only demand rewards on best-sample quality.** Generation 19’s direct-plus-path approach reached **10.54%** mean best gap, versus Generation 15’s direct-demand-only approach at **13.80%**. Generation 15 still had 100% feasibility, so the improvement is associated with adding supported path guidance rather than simply maximizing feasibility.

## Ineffective Approaches
- **Broad or less selective path terms did not match the sparse formulations.** Generation 14’s capped-demand blend and limited relay bonuses scored **0.78**, with an **18.06%** mean best gap. Generation 15 and Generation 17, each with **480 terms**, scored **0.80 / 13.80%** and **0.81 / 12.94%**, respectively—behind Generation 19’s 0.82 / 10.54%.
- **Extra load or endpoint heuristics have not produced consistent gains.** Generation 17’s shared-endpoint penalties scored **0.81** with a **12.94%** best gap and 97% feasibility; Generation 18’s relay-load discount scored **0.81** with an **11.47%** gap and 100% feasibility. Both were outperformed on best gap by Generation 19’s simpler direct-plus-selective-path formulation.
- **A fixed penalty increase and capped direct-demand blending were not beneficial in the reported comparisons.** Generation 14 used penalty weight **1.5** and scored **0.78**, while Generation 19 uses **1.0** and scored **0.82**. This is consistent with earlier results where Generation 10’s 1.5 penalty and broader terms trailed Generation 11’s normalized, selective formulation.

## Implementation Insights
- **Generation 19 clips negative demand, clears the diagonal, and scales demand by its maximum before constructing coefficients.** It gives every directed non-self link a linear coefficient of \(-0.8t_{ij}\), preserving a simple demand-proportional direct-link signal.
- **Its path construction filters on endpoint demand and leg support.** For a path \(i \to j \to k\), it requires positive \(t_{ik}\), inbound support \(t_{ij}/\mathrm{colsum}_j\), and outbound support \(t_{jk}/\mathrm{rowsum}_j\). It then caps the scaled geometric-mean profile at 1 and applies a **0.16 × endpoint-demand × profile** bonus. This selective design produces **211** terms, matching Generation 11’s sparse term count and contrasting with the 480-term alternatives.
- **The current program maintains fast construction and high sample validity without maximizing either metric.** It builds in approximately **0.00s**, produces **64 distinct topologies** per case, and has a **98% mean feasible rate**. Generation 15 reported 100% feasibility but worse best-gap quality; the current program’s advantage is its stronger objective guidance, not runtime or topology diversity.

## Performance Analysis
- **Generation 19 is the current leader by mean best gap among the reported programs:** **10.54%** at combined score **0.82**. It improves on Generation 11 (**11.39%, 0.82**) and Generation 18 (**11.47%, 0.81**). The score ties Generation 11, so the clearest measured gain is best-solution quality rather than the combined score.
- **Its case results are consistently better than the reference but vary materially by instance.** The smallest gaps are on s0 (**8.0%**) and s1 (**8.5%**); s2 is hardest at **13.4%**, followed by s3 at **12.3%**. Its 13.4% worst-case gap is still below the worst gaps reported for several alternatives, including Generation 14’s 20.9% and Generation 17’s 14.5%.
- **Best-sample quality remains much stronger than typical-sample quality.** Generation 19’s mean median gap is **36.06%**, over 25 percentage points above its **10.54%** mean best gap. This is similar to Generation 11’s **35.91%** median versus **11.39%** best gap: both formulations can find strong samples, while their typical samples remain substantially worse.
- **Feasibility is strong but not perfect, and is not the primary differentiator.** Generation 19 averages **98%** feasibility (97–100% across cases), compared with 100% for Generations 14, 15, and 18. Its lower mean best gap despite slightly imperfect feasibility reinforces the observed pattern that selective demand-supported path rewards, rather than feasibility rate alone, track the strongest solution quality.

# META RECOMMENDATIONS

The following are actionable recommendations for the next program generations:

1. **Sweep the direct-reward and path-bonus balance while preserving the current sparse path filters.** Test `direct_coefficient` at `0.65` and `1.0` against the current `0.8`, and the path coefficient at `0.08`, `0.16`, and `0.20`. Generation 19’s selective direct-plus-path formulation leads on mean best gap, so tuning these weights is a low-risk way to improve its signal balance without broadening the 211-term path set.

2. **Ablate or reduce path rewards specifically on `network10_s2`.** Compare the current path coefficient with a reduced value and zero, leaving all other construction unchanged; assess whether weaker path guidance improves the hardest case’s 13.4% gap without degrading the other cases. Selective path rewards beat direct-only approaches overall, so treat this as a targeted diagnostic rather than a general removal of path terms.

3. **Test bounded alternatives to maximum demand normalization while keeping the interaction structure fixed.** Compare the current maximum scaling with scaling by the mean positive off-diagonal demand, and with a clipped-maximum scale. This tests whether a few high-demand links are compressing useful demand signal on weaker cases while retaining the proven selective support conditions.

4. **Tune the support-profile strength without adding paths.** Keep the eligibility checks for positive endpoint demand and both supported legs, but compare `min(1, n * sqrt(in_support * out_support))` with a smaller cap scale and with a profile exponent of `0.75`. The current sparse support-weighted construction outperformed broader 480-term variants, so vary reward strength rather than relaxing the filters.

5. **Compare the current two-leg support profile with a route-specific bounded support factor.** For each existing eligible path \(i \to j \to k\), try weighting its bonus by a capped function of the particular leg shares (for example, their minimum) instead of their geometric mean, while keeping the endpoint-demand multiplier and path set unchanged. This isolates whether the current profile best identifies credible relays without introducing the extra load or endpoint heuristics that have not shown consistent gains.
