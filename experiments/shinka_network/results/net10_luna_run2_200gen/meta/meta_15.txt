# GLOBAL INSIGHTS SCRATCHPAD

The following are global insights about optimization approaches and their effectiveness:

## Successful Algorithmic Patterns
- **Generation 14, “stronger_two_hop,” is the current leader among the listed programs**, scoring **0.82** with a **10.53% mean best gap**, **35.22% mean median gap**, and **99% feasibility**. Its direct-demand linear rewards are complemented by negative two-hop terms for demand-supported paths, discounted by transit-node pressure and the demand on the path’s edges.
- **Demand-gated, load-discounted two-hop rewards remain the strongest pattern for best-sample quality.** Generation 14’s stronger path term improves on Generation 12’s similar demand-and-pressure surrogate (**0.81**, **12.58%** best gap) and Generation 13 (**0.80**, **13.96%**). It also improves on the previous Generation 10 leader’s **11.01%** best gap, though the stated implementations differ in their route-reward details.
- **Direct demand remains a useful foundation when paired with complementary routing signals.** Generation 11’s direct-plus-two-hop formulation scored **0.80** with a **14.31%** best gap; Generation 14’s direct-demand reward plus stronger discounted path rewards achieved a substantially smaller best gap. The results support the combined signal, not simply direct-demand weighting alone.

## Ineffective Approaches
- **Penalizing similar or shared-endpoint links did not match the gains from path rewards.** Generation 8’s diversity penalty scored **0.80** with a **14.12%** best gap and **39.16%** median gap. Its 720 quadratic terms and 64 distinct topologies per case did not translate into better objective quality; the feedback identifies network10_s1 as its weakest case.
- **The lighter endpoint-pressure mixtures were less effective than Generation 14.** Generation 12 scored **0.81** with a **12.58%** best gap, while Generation 13 scored **0.80** with **13.96%**. Both had 480 quadratic terms and high feasibility, but their results were weaker than Generation 14’s **10.53%** gap.
- **High feasibility and topology diversity do not ensure good typical samples.** Generation 14’s median gap is still **35.22%**, despite 99% feasibility and 64 distinct topologies per case. Similar median-gap weakness appears in Generations 8–13, so the best-sample gains do not extend comparably to the median.

## Implementation Insights
- **Generation 14’s linear term is simple and demand-focused:** each directed link gets weight `-demand[i,j] / max(demand)`. Its path terms apply only when the corresponding OD demand is positive, avoiding rewards for unsupported routes.
- **The path reward is specifically discounted twice:** by intermediate-node incident demand relative to mean incident demand, with a lower bound of `0.75`, and by the two edges’ combined direct demand through `1 + 0.5 * edge_pressure`. The path coefficient uses `path_strength = 0.20` and scales with normalized through-demand. This yields 480 quadratic terms on the tested 10-node cases.
- **A fixed degree-penalty weight of 1.0 accompanies a compact, fast construction.** Generation 14 builds in effectively **0.00 seconds**, has **99%** mean feasibility, and reports 98–100% validity before repair across cases. Its score advantage therefore comes with no apparent runtime or term-count cost relative to the 480-term Generations 11–13.
- **Pressure-aware path rewards are not automatically sufficient:** Generation 11 also used pressure-aware two-hop rewards and had 480 terms, but scored **0.80** with a **14.31%** best gap. The results distinguish Generation 14’s stronger two-hop formulation and explicit edge-pressure discount from the broader path-reward pattern.

## Performance Analysis
- **Generation 14 has the best mean best gap in the current comparisons:** **10.53%**, versus Generation 10’s previously reported **11.01%** and Generation 12’s **12.58%**. It ties the top reported score at **0.82** (previously also achieved by Generation 5 and Generation 10) and has the lowest best gap among these variants.
- **Its gains are consistent but instance-dependent.** Generation 14’s best gaps range from **7.2%** on `network10_s3` to **13.3%** on `network10_s2`; `s2` is its weakest case. This differs from Generation 10, whose weakest case was `s1` at **13.5%**. The current best improves the mean but does not eliminate case-specific variation.
- **Generation 14’s median gap is better than the previous Generation 10 figure, but remains large:** **35.22%** versus **35.91%**. Its per-case median gaps span **32.4–37.0%**, showing that the typical-sample limitation persists despite improved best-sample performance.
- **Across the new variants, term count and diversity are poor predictors of quality.** Generation 8 had 720 terms and scored **0.80**, while Generations 11–14 had 480 terms and scored **0.80–0.82**; all reported 64 distinct topologies per case. Performance differences align more closely with the form and weighting of demand and path rewards than with term count or topology count.

# META RECOMMENDATIONS

The following are actionable recommendations for the next program generations:

1. **Sweep the two-hop reward strength around the current `path_strength = 0.20`.** Test values such as 0.15, 0.25, and 0.30 while keeping demand gating, transit-node pressure, and edge-pressure discounting unchanged. The current pressure-aware path formulation gives the best observed mean best gap, so tuning its magnitude is a focused way to improve the weaker `network10_s2` result without discarding the successful structure.

2. **Test alternative bounded transit-node pressure normalizations.** Keep the monotonic discount, but compare the current incident-demand-to-mean ratio with a ratio to maximum incident demand, using a floor such as 0.75 in each case. This preserves the successful penalty on heavily loaded intermediate nodes while testing whether the current global-average reference over- or under-discounts paths on unevenly loaded instances.

3. **Strengthen path plausibility using both component edges’ direct demand.** Replace the current edge-pressure discount with a bounded factor that also rewards well-supported route edges—for example, multiply the path reward by the square root of the product of the two normalized edge demands, while retaining a nonzero floor. This tests whether two-hop rewards are more useful when both links are plausible, extending the current edge-aware path signal without adding quadratic terms.

4. **Try a mild concave transform of direct-demand rewards while retaining the two-hop terms.** Compare the current linear link reward with a blend such as 80% linear and 20% square-root normalized demand. Direct demand is a useful foundation, and this variation may give moderate-demand links more influence, potentially addressing the large median gap without weakening the proven path structure.

5. **Tune the edge-pressure discount separately from the path coefficient.** Compare the current `1 + 0.5 * edge_pressure` denominator with nearby strengths, such as 0.25 and 0.75, while leaving `path_strength` fixed. This isolates how strongly direct traffic on route edges should suppress an indirect reward and may improve the best-sample quality while keeping the compact 480-term formulation.
