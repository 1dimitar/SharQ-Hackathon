# GLOBAL INSIGHTS SCRATCHPAD

The following are global insights about optimization approaches and their effectiveness:

## Successful Algorithmic Patterns
- **The current best, Generation 14 “stronger_two_hop,” remains the leader:** score **0.82**, mean best gap **10.53%**, median gap **35.22%**, and **99%** feasibility. Its strongest pattern is demand-based direct-link rewards combined with demand-gated two-hop rewards that account for both transit-node pressure and the demand on the two route edges.
- **Demand-supported path rewards consistently outperform simpler demand mixtures.** Generation 14’s **10.53%** best gap is better than the new variants: Generation 17 “capacity_weighted_route_potential” (**12.05%**), Generation 18 “gated_twohop_support” (**12.07%**), Generation 16 “path_balance_surrogate” (**12.40%**), Generation 15 “endpoint_pressure_mix” (**13.03%**), and Generation 21 “bounded_transit_surrogate” (**13.50%**). This reinforces the earlier finding that the *form and weighting* of the path reward matter more than simply including a two-hop term.
- **Direct-demand rewards are a useful foundation when complemented by targeted routing incentives.** Generation 14 normalizes direct demand for its linear terms and rewards two-hop routes only for positive-demand OD pairs. The new programs also generally use demand-based linear rewards, but their weaker best gaps suggest that endpoint-load mixing, activity weighting, or other path adjustments did not improve on Generation 14’s specific pressure-and-edge-discount formulation.

## Ineffective Approaches
- **Endpoint or activity weighting alone did not close the gap to Generation 14.** Generation 15’s 90% direct-demand/10% endpoint-load blend scored **0.80** with a **13.03%** best gap; Generation 17’s direct-demand and active-endpoint weighting scored **0.81** with a **12.05%** gap. Both trail Generation 14’s **10.53%** gap.
- **Traffic-profile similarity penalties did not help typical or best quality.** Generation 16 includes penalties for link pairs with similar traffic profiles, yet scored **0.81** with a **12.40%** best gap and **37.11%** median gap. The earlier Generation 8 diversity-penalty result likewise scored **0.80** with a **14.12%** best gap. These results do not support similarity penalties as a substitute for well-targeted path rewards.
- **High feasibility and many distinct samples are not sufficient for strong objective quality.** The new programs mostly produced 64 distinct topologies per case and had near-perfect or perfect feasibility, but their best gaps were **12.05–13.50%** and median gaps about **36.67–38.96%**. Generation 14 also has 64 distinct topologies and 99% feasibility, but substantially better best-sample quality.

## Implementation Insights
- **Generation 14’s path reward is selective and pressure-aware.** It skips OD pairs with nonpositive demand, scales rewards by normalized through-demand, discounts by intermediate-node incident demand relative to its mean (with a **0.75** lower bound), and further discounts by the two path edges’ demand through `1 + 0.5 * edge_pressure`. The resulting formulation has **480 quadratic terms** and the best reported mean gap.
- **Its compact implementation is fast without sacrificing reported quality.** Generation 14 builds in effectively **0.00 seconds**, uses a fixed degree-penalty weight of **1.0**, and reaches a **10.53%** mean best gap. The new variants also build in under **0.01 seconds** (or 0.00 seconds where reported), so runtime does not explain their weaker scores.
- **Adding more quadratic structure did not yield better results.** Generation 16 uses **1,200 quadratic terms**, but its **12.40%** best gap is worse than Generation 14’s 480-term formulation. Generation 15, 17, 18, and 21 also use 480 terms yet score below Generation 14, underscoring that term count alone is not predictive.
- **The current best’s remaining weakness is typical-sample quality and case variation.** Its median gaps range from **32.4% to 37.0%** despite best gaps of **7.2% to 13.3%**. This best-versus-median spread is also present in the new programs, indicating that stronger best samples do not translate into similarly strong typical samples.

## Performance Analysis
- **Generation 14 remains ahead of every newly reported variant on mean best gap.** Its **10.53%** gap beats Generation 17 (**12.05%**), Generation 18 (**12.07%**), Generation 16 (**12.40%**), Generation 15 (**13.03%**), and Generation 21 (**13.50%**). Its score of **0.82** also exceeds the new programs’ **0.80–0.81** scores.
- **The newer variants show a modest cluster rather than a clear improvement over the leader.** Generations 16–18 all scored **0.81**, with best gaps between **12.05% and 12.40%**; Generation 15 scored **0.80**, and Generation 21 also scored **0.80**. Their different weighting schemes did not surpass Generation 14’s path formulation.
- **Generation 14’s weakest case is `network10_s2` at +13.3%, but it still beats the new programs’ reported ranges overall.** Its best gap spans **7.2–13.3%**. By comparison, Generation 17 spans **8.9–14.1%**, Generation 18 **9.7–14.1%**, Generation 16 **9.4–15.7%**, Generation 15 **6.3–19.4%**, and Generation 21 **11.3–14.5%**. The per-case results show variation, but not a consistent new leader.
- **Median gaps remain comparatively high across all approaches.** Generation 14’s **35.22%** mean median gap is better than Generation 15 (**38.96%**), Generation 16 (**37.11%**), Generation 17 (**37.55%**), Generation 18 (**36.67%**), and Generation 21 (**37.37%**), but the margin is smaller than its best-gap advantage. Its best-sample gains therefore do not eliminate the broader typical-sample limitation.

# META RECOMMENDATIONS

The following are actionable recommendations for the next program generations:

1. **Sweep the two-hop reward strength around the current value of 0.20.** Test 0.15, 0.25, and 0.30 while keeping positive-demand gating, transit pressure, and edge-pressure discounting unchanged. This isolates the scale of the path reward, the central feature of the best-performing formulation, and may improve the weaker `network10_s2` case.

2. **Compare bounded transit-pressure normalizations.** Keep the current inverse pressure discount and 0.75 floor, but normalize incident demand by the maximum rather than the mean; optionally test a floor of 0.85 alongside 0.75. This preserves the demonstrated benefit of avoiding overloaded transit nodes while testing whether the mean reference over-penalizes paths in instances with uneven node loads.

3. **Tune the edge-pressure discount independently of path strength.** Replace the current denominator factor `1 + 0.5 * edge_pressure` with `1 + α * edge_pressure`, testing values such as α = 0.25 and 0.75 while holding other terms fixed. Generation 14’s edge-aware path reward outperforms simpler path rewards, so calibrating how strongly busy route edges suppress indirect rewards is a focused extension.

4. **Test a bounded route-edge support multiplier in place of part of the edge-pressure discount.** For each eligible two-hop path, multiply its reward by a factor with a nonzero floor that increases with the normalized demands on both route edges—for example, a blend of the current discount and the square root of their demand product. This tests whether favoring paths with supported component links can improve on pressure discounting alone without adding quadratic terms.

5. **Introduce a small concave component into the direct-link reward while retaining the proven path terms.** Blend the current normalized linear demand reward with its square root, for example 80% linear and 20% square-root, and leave the two-hop formulation unchanged initially. This preserves direct demand as the foundation while giving moderate-demand links more influence, a targeted test for improving typical-sample quality without replacing the successful path structure.
