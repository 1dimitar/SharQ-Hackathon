# GLOBAL INSIGHTS SCRATCHPAD

The following are global insights about optimization approaches and their effectiveness:

## Successful Algorithmic Patterns
- **The current best, Generation 109 (“Pressure-Aware Sparse QUBO Surrogate”), remains the strongest evaluated program:** score **0.87**, mean best gap **9.72%**, and mean median gap **22.08%**. Its demand-sensitive direct rewards and selectively ranked, pressure-aware relay paths improve on the earlier Generation 28 leader (score **0.85**, gaps **10.86% / 25.17%**).
- **Selective relay allocation remains more effective than broad or uniformly sparse relay construction.** Generation 109 gives high-demand OD pairs a larger relay budget and ranks relays using leg affinity adjusted for node pressure. By comparison, Generation 106 retains one relay per pair and up to two for high-demand pairs (score **0.81**, best gap **13.56%**), while Generation 108 uses top-two-or-four relay candidates (score **0.77**, best gap **18.66%**).
- **Integrated demand and congestion signals are associated with the best solution quality.** Generation 109 combines normalized linear-plus-square-root direct rewards with a two-hop support reward and relay-pressure discount. This outperforms the newer congestion-share Generation 105 (score **0.80**, best gap **17.56%**) and sparse pressure-aware Generation 107 (score **0.78**, best gap **19.09%**).

## Ineffective Approaches
- **Congestion or pressure adjustments did not help when they were less selective or less well integrated.** Generation 105’s congestion-weighted two-hop rewards and evaluator-applied degree constraints scored **0.80**, with a **17.56%** mean best gap and only **16%** feasibility. Its `network12_s0` validity was just **6%** before repair. Generation 108’s pressure-discounted rewards scored **0.77**, with an **18.66%** best gap and **10%** feasibility.
- **A small relay budget alone did not ensure either good solutions or feasible samples.** Generation 106 produced **72–78 distinct topologies**, but had only **5%** mean pre-repair feasibility and a **13.56%** best gap. Generation 107’s 200-term sparse model still had **19.09%** mean best gap, despite **23%** feasibility.
- **Generation 109’s strong objective quality does not translate to reliable feasibility.** It has the best score and gaps among these programs, yet only **5%** mean pre-repair feasibility (2% on s0, 7% on s1). Its 100 samples per instance yielded **100 and 91 distinct topologies**, showing broad exploration but little concentration on valid samples.

## Implementation Insights
- **Generation 109 preserves the effective direct-link reward shape:** it clips negative demand, zeros the diagonal, normalizes by maximum demand, and assigns each off-diagonal link `-(0.75*d + 0.25*sqrt(d))`. This balances emphasis on large demands with a concave contribution from smaller positive demands.
- **Its relay selection jointly scores leg affinity and relay pressure.** For each positive-demand OD pair, it ranks candidates using `(min(a,b) + 0.15*max(a,b) + 0.01) / pressure`, where pressure reflects normalized incoming/outgoing node demand and is increased by the candidate legs’ demand. This favors relays supported by both legs while discounting loaded nodes.
- **Relay capacity is demand-adaptive, not uniformly expanded.** The program uses a base budget of `min(n-2, max(1, n//5))` and adds up to two choices for OD demands at or above the 80th percentile. The resulting **216 quadratic terms** are fewer than Generation 28’s **304**, yet Generation 109 achieves better gaps; term count alone therefore does not explain quality.
- **Good best-solution performance and feasibility are distinct outcomes.** Generation 109 returns penalty weight **1.5**, but only 2–7% of samples are valid before repair. The other programs also show weak feasibility across varying fixed penalties, supporting the previous observation that the reward and coupling structure—not penalty weight alone—determines validity.

## Performance Analysis
- **Generation 109 leads both the earlier and newly reported programs on aggregate score and solution gaps:** score **0.87**, gaps **9.72% / 22.08%**. It improves on Generation 28’s **0.85** and **10.86% / 25.17%**, and substantially outperforms Generations 105–108, whose scores range from **0.77 to 0.81** and best gaps from **13.56% to 19.09%**.
- **Its best gaps are consistent across the two network12 cases:** **9.2%** on s0 and **10.2%** on s1; median gaps are **22.3%** and **21.8%**. The larger best gap is on s1, but the spread is modest compared with the larger case-to-case swings noted for several earlier variants.
- **Among the recent variants, Generation 106 is the closest on best-solution gap** at **13.56%**, but its median gap (**34.41%**) and feasibility (**5%**) are worse than Generation 109’s. Generation 105 has **16%** feasibility but weaker gaps (**17.56% / 33.39%**), illustrating that higher feasibility does not necessarily correspond to better objective quality.
- **Quadratic-term count has no simple relationship with score.** Generation 109’s 216 terms outperform Generation 105’s 352 and Generation 107’s 200, while Generation 108’s 220 terms score only **0.77**. Relay ranking, demand-sensitive budgeting, and how pressure affects rewards appear more consequential than raw sparsity.

# META RECOMMENDATIONS

The following are actionable recommendations for the next program generations:

1. **Concentrate extra relay capacity on the strongest OD demands.** Raise the extra-relay cutoff from the 80th to the 90th percentile, keeping the base budget and two-extra cap unchanged. This tests whether focusing the existing model capacity on the most important pairs improves solution quality without increasing the number of terms.

2. **Favor relays with a strong weaker leg.** Replace the current affinity `min(a, b) + 0.15 * max(a, b) + 0.01` with `min(a, b) + 0.25 * max(a, b)`, leaving pressure adjustment and relay budgets fixed. The leader’s selective path ranking is effective; this isolates whether a modestly different balance between leg strength and the weaker leg improves relay choices.

3. **Tune the strength of pressure-aware relay selection.** Change the candidate ranking denominator from `pressure` to `pressure**p`, testing `p = 0.75` and `p = 1.25` as separate variants while keeping the relay reward unchanged. This probes whether the current pressure discount is too aggressive or too weak without altering the successful demand-sensitive reward structure.

4. **Make relay rewards more selective by OD demand.** Multiply the existing relay reward by `1.1` for OD pairs at or above the current 80th-percentile cutoff and by `0.9` for other positive-demand pairs. This complements the leader’s high-demand relay allocation by prioritizing those interactions in the objective, while retaining its direct-link rewards and pressure adjustment.

5. **Test a modestly stronger two-hop support reward.** Increase the relay coefficient from `0.30` to `0.35`, keeping the support formula, candidate ranking, and relay budgets fixed. Generation 109’s integrated direct and relay rewards outperform the less selective variants, so a small coefficient adjustment can test whether its successful two-hop signal is underweighted without making a broad structural change.
