# GLOBAL INSIGHTS SCRATCHPAD

The following are global insights about optimization approaches and their effectiveness:

## Successful Algorithmic Patterns
- **The current best remains the strongest overall design:** Generation 5, `sparse_relay_support`, scores **0.85**, with an **11.36% mean best gap**, **25.86% median gap**, and **264 quadratic terms**. It combines a linear-plus-square-root demand reward (`0.75*value + 0.25*sqrt(value)`) with a bounded set of relays ranked by two-leg affinity and node pressure.
- **Sparse, pressure-aware relay ranking consistently beats broad support, though details matter.** Generation 5 uses balanced leg affinity, `sqrt((0.1+a)*(0.1+b))`, discounted by relay pressure, and selects a limited relay set. By contrast, broad-support models with 880 terms scored only **0.74–0.79** in earlier evaluations. The new sparse variants confirm that merely limiting terms is not enough: Generation 20’s bottleneck-aware design scored **0.83** with 188 terms, below Generation 5.
- **Demand blending remains a useful component of strong models.** Generation 5 applies the same linear/square-root blend to direct-link rewards and OD path support. New programs using similar blends achieved scores of **0.82–0.84** (Generations 9, 14, 18, and 19), supporting the blend’s value, but not matching the current best’s combined score.
- **Best-found quality and typical-sample quality are distinct.** Generation 5 has the best combined score and a **25.86%** median gap, while Generation 14 reaches a slightly better **30.99%** median gap only in a different tradeoff; Generation 18 has a **27.69%** median gap. The current best’s strongest evidence is its combination of score and best-gap performance, not uniformly superior metrics.

## Ineffective Approaches
- **Increasing the number of relay interactions has not reliably improved results.** Generation 18’s 308-term model scored **0.84** with an **11.81%** mean best gap; Generation 14’s 264-term model scored **0.83** with an **11.64%** gap. Neither exceeds Generation 5’s **0.85** score, despite comparable or greater interaction counts.
- **Changing relay scoring or selection while retaining sparse support can degrade quality.** Generation 9 uses up to two relays and a degree-penalty weight of 4.0; it scored **0.82**, with **11.59%** best gap and **35.22%** median gap. Generation 19’s sparse 176-term design scored **0.83** with a **12.20%** best gap. Generation 20’s bottleneck-aware selection scored **0.83** and had a worse **13.55%** best gap. These results reinforce that sparsity alone does not reproduce Generation 5’s ranking-and-reward combination.
- **More relay candidates can worsen feasibility without improving score.** Generation 14 adds relay terms for a broader set of candidates, yielding 264 terms but only **1%** mean pre-repair feasibility; Generation 18 has 308 terms and **14%** feasibility. Generation 5 has just **8%** feasibility, so these figures do not establish that more terms solve validity; they instead show that feasibility and score vary independently.
- **Penalty-weight changes have not consistently helped.** The new programs use weights from **1.5** (Generation 14) to **5.0** (Generation 19), with scores from **0.82 to 0.84** and feasibility from **1% to 15%**. Earlier results likewise found no reliable relationship between penalty magnitude and quality; Generation 5’s weight of **1.5** supports the best combined score, despite low raw feasibility.

## Implementation Insights
- **Generation 5’s relay budget is explicitly bounded per demanded OD pair.** For each positive-demand pair it selects `min(n-2, max(2, n//4))` relays, producing **264 quadratic terms** on network12. This keeps the model compact relative to the 880-term broad-support variants while retaining the best combined score.
- **Its ranking uses both path-leg plausibility and transit pressure.** The program computes affinity as `sqrt((0.1+a)*(0.1+b))`, divides by `0.75 + node_pressure`, and defines pressure from the larger of normalized incoming and outgoing demand, with a **0.65 floor**. This differs from approaches that rank chiefly by demand quantile, edge load, or generic bottleneck pressure; the evaluations show those alternatives did not surpass Generation 5.
- **The code applies consistent preprocessing and reward construction.** It clips negative demand, clears the diagonal, normalizes by maximum traffic, and uses the same linear/square-root blend for direct rewards and path support. It sorts the two edge keys before insertion, canonicalizing quadratic terms and avoiding duplicate representations.
- **High-quality best samples coexist with poor raw feasibility in the current best.** Generation 5’s two network12 cases have best gaps of **+10.0%** and **+12.8%**, but only **10%** and **6%** of samples are valid before repair. The second case also has fewer distinct topologies (**62** versus **93**) and the larger gap, while both cases have 264 terms and 6,012 circuits.

## Performance Analysis
- **Generation 5 remains the overall leader among the reported programs:** score **0.85**, mean best gap **11.36%**, and median gap **25.86%**. The closest new result is Generation 18 at **0.84**, with an **11.81%** best gap, **27.69%** median gap, **14%** feasibility, and 308 terms.
- **The new sparse variants generally trail the current best despite compact models.** Generation 9 scores **0.82** with 176 terms; Generation 14 scores **0.83** with 264 terms; Generation 19 scores **0.83** with 176 terms; and Generation 20 scores **0.83** with 188 terms. Their mean best gaps range from **11.59% to 13.55%**, versus Generation 5’s **11.36%**.
- **The best score does not correspond to the best metric in every category.** Generation 14 has the lowest new mean best gap (**11.64%**, close to Generation 5) but only a **0.83** score and **1%** feasibility. Generation 20 has the highest new feasibility (**28%**) but a **13.55%** best gap and **0.83** score. Generation 5’s lead is therefore a combined-performance result rather than dominance on every individual metric.
- **Across the new two-case feedback, case difficulty and sampling outcomes remain uneven.** Generation 5’s best gap increases from **+10.0%** on `network12_s0` to **+12.8%** on `network12_s1`; Generation 9 similarly has gaps of **+13.1%** and **+10.1%**, while Generation 20 reports **+12.9–14.2%**. Pre-repair feasibility also varies sharply within programs, so aggregate rates conceal substantial case-level differences.

# META RECOMMENDATIONS

The following are actionable recommendations for the next program generations:

1. **Sweep the relay budget while keeping the current ranking and weights fixed.** Compare `max(1, n // 5)`, the current `max(2, n // 4)`, and `n // 3` relays per positive-demand OD pair, each capped at `n - 2`. This directly tests whether a small support change can improve the weaker `network12_s1` case without returning to broad support.

2. **Allocate extra relays selectively to high-demand OD pairs.** Keep the current relay budget for most pairs, but add one or two candidates for pairs in the top demand quantile, with a strict overall cap on added terms. This tests whether targeted support can improve the largest observed gap while avoiding the unreliable cost of expanding support for every pair.

3. **Test a more conservative two-leg affinity in the relay ranking.** Replace `sqrt((0.1+a)*(0.1+b))` with a minimum-leg-sensitive score such as `sqrt((0.1+min(a,b))*(0.1+max(a,b)))`, leaving the pressure discount and relay budget unchanged. The current best favors balanced legs; this variation tests whether further suppressing relays with one weak leg improves path quality.

4. **Tune how strongly node pressure discounts relay choices.** Keep the current pressure definition and compare ranking and weight denominators based on `(0.75 + pressure) ** α` for a few values around `α = 1`, applying each value consistently in both places. Pressure-aware sparse ranking is part of the strongest design, while other pressure formulations have not surpassed it, so this isolates the strength of the successful mechanism.

5. **Tune direct and two-hop reward balance independently.** Preserve the linear-plus-square-root shape, but first test small changes around the current `0.75/0.25` direct-link blend; then, in separate variants, adjust the quadratic multiplier around `0.25`. Demand blending has performed well across several programs, and changing one component at a time will show whether the current balance leaves useful direct-link or relay support underweighted.
