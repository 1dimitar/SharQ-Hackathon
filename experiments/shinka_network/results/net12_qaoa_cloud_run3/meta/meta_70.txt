# GLOBAL INSIGHTS SCRATCHPAD

The following are global insights about optimization approaches and their effectiveness:

## Successful Algorithmic Patterns
- **Generation 28 `targeted_high_demand_relays` remains the top reported result at 0.85.** Its direct-link blend, pressure-aware relay ranking, and extra relay allocation for high-demand OD pairs achieved a 10.86% mean best gap and 25.17% mean median gap. Its score is tied with Generations 58, 62, and 63, but its reported mean gaps are lower than those peers.
- **The current best’s targeted relay construction is more effective than uniform relay expansion in the reported comparisons.** It uses a base relay budget plus up to two relays for OD pairs above the 80th-percentile demand cutoff. Generation 60’s broader relay-budget approach scored 0.67, with 34.26% mean best and 70.18% mean median gaps.
- **Compact, pressure-aware relay selection can remain competitive.** Generation 58 `bottleneck_relay_ranking` matched the current best’s 0.85 score using 176 quadratic terms rather than 304, with a 10.91% mean best gap and 26.28% median gap. Its results support selective relay interactions, though the current best’s reported gaps are slightly lower.
- **Direct-link reward calibration is a recurring successful pattern.** The current best uses `-(0.75*d + 0.25*sqrt(d))`; Generation 62 `direct_heavy_blend` also scored 0.85, with an 11.05% mean best gap and 25.55% median gap. This blend rewards large demands while preserving some contribution from moderate demands.

## Ineffective Approaches
- **The new sparse and diversity-focused variants did not match the current best’s solution quality.** Generation 57 `diverse_neighborhoods` scored 0.82 (12.16% mean best gap, 33.63% median gap); Generation 66 `aggregated_relay_qubo` scored 0.82 (15.15%, 30.39%); and Generation 67 `gentler_relay_pressure` scored 0.81 (15.16%, 32.99%). Their reported gaps show that diversity terms or sparse relay support alone did not reproduce the current best’s reward calibration and targeted selection.
- **Sparse pressure-discounted relay models frequently had very low pre-repair feasibility.** Generation 66 had 4% mean feasibility, Generation 71 `sparse_relay_balance` had 5%, and Generation 57 had 10%, compared with 17% for Generation 28. Generation 70 `stronger_degree_feasibility` improved feasibility to 19%, but scored 0.82 and had a 12.97% mean best gap and 32.29% median gap.
- **Increasing interaction count did not reliably improve results.** Generation 62 achieved 0.85 with 880 terms but only 6% feasibility, while Generation 60 used 352 terms and scored 0.67. Conversely, Generation 58 matched 0.85 with 176 terms. Term count alone does not explain quality or feasibility.
- **High topology diversity did not guarantee good solutions.** Generations 66 and 67 produced 90–96 distinct topologies but had mean best gaps around 15% and feasibility of 4–24%. The current best likewise sampled 80 and 86 distinct topologies, yet only 20% and 14% of samples were valid; diversity is not a substitute for constraint satisfaction or low objective gaps.

## Implementation Insights
- **The current best combines a calibrated direct reward with demand-weighted relay support.** It clips negative demand, removes diagonal traffic, normalizes by maximum demand, and assigns each off-diagonal direct link `-(0.75*d + 0.25*sqrt(d))`. For positive-demand OD pairs, it uses the same blend as path support, which ties relay rewards to OD importance. This implementation corresponds to 10.86% mean best and 25.17% mean median gaps.
- **Its relay ranking incorporates both leg affinity and node pressure.** Candidate relays are ranked by `sqrt((0.1+a)*(0.1+b)) / (0.75 + pressure[j])`, with pressure based on the larger normalized incoming or outgoing load. The program then applies a mild rank factor and another pressure discount to the relay interaction weight. This is more targeted than simply selecting sparse relays, as illustrated by the weaker results for Generations 66, 67, and 71.
- **Relay allocation is demand-selective rather than uniform.** Generation 28 uses `min(n-2, max(2, n//4))` relays by default and adds up to two for OD pairs at or above the 80th-percentile positive-demand cutoff. Its 304 terms produced the lowest reported mean gaps among the tied 0.85 programs, while Generation 58 achieved a similar score with 176 terms but slightly higher gaps.
- **The evaluator supplies degree penalties, while Generation 28 returns a fixed weight of 1.5.** This yielded 17% mean pre-repair feasibility, better than several sparse alternatives (4–10% for Generations 57, 66, and 71), but still left most samples invalid. Its case-level validity was 20% on `network12_s0` and 14% on `network12_s1`.

## Performance Analysis
- **Generation 28 is the reported overall leader at 0.85, tied with Generations 58, 62, and 63.** Its mean best gap of 10.86% and median gap of 25.17% are lower than Generation 58’s 10.91% and 26.28%, and Generation 62’s 11.05% and 25.55%. The score tie does not mean equivalent gaps or feasibility.
- **Against the new evaluations, Generation 28’s margins are clearest in typical-solution quality and score.** The new programs scored 0.81–0.82, with mean median gaps from 30.39% to 35.50%, versus 25.17% for Generation 28. Their mean best gaps ranged from 12.16% to 15.16%, versus 10.86%.
- **Generation 28’s case-level quality is reasonably consistent, but `network12_s1` is its weaker case by best gap.** The best gaps are +9.5% on `network12_s0` and +12.3% on `network12_s1`; median gaps are +26.5% and +23.9%, respectively. Feasibility also falls from 20% to 14% between the two cases.
- **Quality, feasibility, and sparsity do not move together reliably.** Generation 70 reached 19% feasibility—slightly above Generation 28’s 17% mean—but scored 0.82 with a 32.29% median gap. Generation 58 used fewer terms and matched 0.85, whereas Generation 66’s 176 terms coincided with a 0.82 score and 30.39% median gap. The combined results favor evaluating these measures together rather than treating any one as a proxy for performance.

# META RECOMMENDATIONS

The following are actionable recommendations for the next program generations:

1. Reduce the ordinary relay budget in a controlled variant: change `relay_count` to `min(n - 2, max(1, n // 5))`, keeping the pressure-aware ranking, direct-link reward, and high-demand extras unchanged. Generation 58 matched the best score with fewer terms, so this tests whether a smaller baseline can retain quality while reducing interactions.

2. Make extra relay allocation more selective by raising `high_demand_cutoff` from the 80th to the 90th percentile, retaining the cap of two extras. This isolates whether concentrating relay support on the strongest OD pairs improves the weaker `network12_s1` best gap without adopting the broadly expanded budgets that performed poorly.

3. Test a balanced-leg affinity in relay ranking: replace the geometric mean with `min(first_leg, second_leg) + 0.25 * max(first_leg, second_leg)`, leaving the pressure discounts and relay budgets fixed. This checks whether requiring meaningful support from both legs improves relay selection while preserving the successful targeted, pressure-aware construction.

4. Sweep relay-pressure discount strength by changing the weight denominator to `(0.75 + node_pressure[j])**alpha`, testing `alpha=0.75` and `alpha=1.25` in separate variants. Pressure-aware ranking is part of the best program’s successful pattern; these isolated tests can reveal whether gentler or stronger discounting improves gaps or feasibility.

5. Tune the direct-link reward independently of relay construction by testing `0.85 * value + 0.15 * sqrt(value)` and `0.65 * value + 0.35 * sqrt(value)` as separate variants. The current `0.75/0.25` blend has the best reported gaps, while these alternatives test whether emphasizing high-demand links or giving moderate demands more influence can improve results.
