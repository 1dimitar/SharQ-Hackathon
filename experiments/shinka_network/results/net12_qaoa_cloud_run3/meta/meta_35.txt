# GLOBAL INSIGHTS SCRATCHPAD

The following are global insights about optimization approaches and their effectiveness:

## Successful Algorithmic Patterns
- **Generation 28, `targeted_high_demand_relays`, remains the strongest evaluated program.** It scores **0.85**, with a **10.86% mean best gap** and **25.17% mean median gap**. Its bounded, targeted relay selection—ordinary support plus up to two extra relays for high-demand OD pairs—performs better than broadly expanding relay support.
- **Pressure-aware, affinity-ranked relay rewards work best when paired with compact support and calibrated direct rewards.** Generation 28 uses the established direct-link blend `0.75*value + 0.25*sqrt(value)` and ranks relays by balanced leg affinity discounted by node pressure. It achieves 304 quadratic terms and outperforms the related Generation 29 and 31 pressure-aware programs, both scoring **0.82–0.84** with larger median gaps.
- **Targeting high-demand OD pairs is a useful refinement over uniform sparse relay selection.** Generation 28’s selective extra-relay budget accompanies better results than Generation 32’s balanced-affinity design (score **0.82**, best gap **14.00%**, median gap **32.32%**) and Generation 29’s limited route portfolio (score **0.82**, median gap **36.77%**).

## Ineffective Approaches
- **Increasing or broadening relay support does not reliably improve quality.** Earlier Generation 30’s expanded budget scored **0.75**, with **20.56% best** and **47.38% median** gaps and only **3% feasibility**. The new Generation 31 pressure-discounted surrogate used **880 quadratic terms** yet scored only **0.84**, below Generation 28’s 304-term result.
- **Sparse relay models without sufficiently effective targeting or calibration have worse solution quality and consistency.** Generation 29 scored **0.82** with a **36.77% median gap**; Generation 32 scored **0.82** with a **32.32% median gap**. Generation 29’s 20% feasibility was close to Generation 28’s 17%, but its much worse median gap shows that feasibility or topology diversity alone does not ensure good objectives.
- **Low raw feasibility remains a weakness even for the best-scoring model.** Generation 28 has only **17% mean pre-repair feasibility** (20% on `network12_s0`, 14% on `network12_s1`). The new valid programs likewise report low rates—**14%** for Generation 31 and **20–21%** for Generations 29 and 32—so strong best-found scores coexist with substantial reliance on repair.
- **Failed evaluations provide no evidence about QUBO quality.** Generations 35 and 38 did not pass validation and had cloud ensemble execution errors (0 of 12 programs completed). Their score of 0.0 cannot be attributed to their relay-selection details based on the reported feedback.

## Implementation Insights
- **Generation 28’s direct-link reward is a well-supported component.** It clips negative demand, clears the diagonal, normalizes by maximum traffic, and applies the `0.75*value + 0.25*sqrt(value)` blend. This matches the pattern associated with Generation 5 and accompanies Generation 28’s leading score of **0.85**.
- **Its relay construction keeps interactions focused.** Candidate relays are ranked by balanced leg affinity, `sqrt((0.1+a)*(0.1+b))`, discounted by node pressure. A size-based base budget is augmented by at most two relays for high-demand OD pairs; the resulting **304 terms** remain compact while supporting targeted paths.
- **Canonicalizing and accumulating quadratic keys makes the interaction model consistent.** Generation 28 sorts each pair of edge keys before insertion and sums contributions that map to the same quadratic key. It leaves degree enforcement to the evaluator and returns penalty weight **1.5**. The results do not isolate the effect of that weight, but the complete compact construction outperforms less targeted alternatives.
- **Pressure-aware ranking alone is not sufficient.** Generation 29 and Generation 31 also incorporate pressure-discounted relay rewards, but report scores of **0.82** and **0.84**, respectively, and median gaps of **36.77%** and **29.22%**. Generation 28’s stronger result is consistent with the combination of calibrated direct rewards, bounded support, and high-demand targeting rather than pressure discounting in isolation.

## Performance Analysis
- **Generation 28 remains the current best:** score **0.85**, mean best gap **10.86%**, and mean median gap **25.17%**. It improves on Generation 31 (**0.84**, **11.62%** best, **29.22%** median) and Generations 29 and 32 (both **0.82**, with median gaps of **36.77%** and **32.32%**).
- **The new results reinforce the importance of targeted interactions over term count.** Generation 31 has **880** quadratic terms and scores **0.84**, while Generation 28 has **304** terms and scores **0.85**. Earlier evidence is similar: Generation 30’s expanded support scored **0.75**, whereas Generation 28’s targeted design scored **0.85**.
- **Generation 28’s case-level outcomes show different strengths across the two instances.** On `network12_s0`, its best gap is **9.5%**, median gap **26.5%**, and raw feasibility **20%**; on `network12_s1`, the best gap is **12.3%**, median gap **23.9%**, and feasibility **14%**. The program’s largest best gap is therefore on `network12_s1`, while its median gap is worse on `network12_s0`.
- **Topology diversity and raw feasibility do not track objective quality consistently.** Generation 28 produces 80 and 86 distinct topologies across the two cases, while the new programs also report many distinct outcomes; nevertheless, their best and median gaps are generally worse. Likewise, Generation 28’s modest feasibility rate accompanies the best score, confirming that these metrics do not by themselves explain solution quality.

# META RECOMMENDATIONS

The following are actionable recommendations for the next program generations:

1. **Sweep the ordinary relay budget while keeping the current ranking and rewards fixed.** Compare `min(n-2, max(1, n//5))`, the current `min(n-2, max(2, n//4))`, and `min(n-2, n//3)`. Generation 28’s compact, targeted support beats broader expansion, so test whether a smaller base budget improves typical solution quality without losing its best results.

2. **Concentrate extra relays on the highest-demand OD pairs.** Keep the base budget unchanged, but raise the cutoff from the 80th percentile to the 90th percentile and retain the cap of two extra relays; optionally cap total quadratic terms to preserve compactness. This directly tests whether focusing support more selectively can improve the weaker best gap on `network12_s1`.

3. **Try a more bottleneck-sensitive relay ranking.** Replace the geometric-mean affinity with `min(first_leg, second_leg) + 0.25 * max(first_leg, second_leg)`, while keeping pressure discounting, budgets, and reward weights unchanged. This favors relays with two useful legs and tests a targeted refinement without expanding support.

4. **Tune pressure sensitivity independently of relay selection.** Keep the current candidate ranking and included features fixed, but change the pressure denominator in the relay reward from `(0.75 + node_pressure)` to `(0.75 + node_pressure)**alpha`, testing `alpha = 0.75` and `1.25`. Pressure-aware designs are competitive but do not outperform the best, so this isolates whether a gentler or stronger pressure penalty improves consistency.

5. **Calibrate direct and relay rewards in separate variants.** First test direct-link blends of `0.85*value + 0.15*sqrt(value)` and `0.65*value + 0.35*sqrt(value)` while leaving relay terms unchanged; then, in separate runs, test `path_strength` values such as `0.22` and `0.34`. The current blend and path strength are part of the best compact design, so controlled one-component changes can reveal whether its remaining gap comes from direct-link preference or relay encouragement.
