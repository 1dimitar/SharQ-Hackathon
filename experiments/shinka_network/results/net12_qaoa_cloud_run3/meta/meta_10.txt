# GLOBAL INSIGHTS SCRATCHPAD

The following are global insights about optimization approaches and their effectiveness:

## Successful Algorithmic Patterns
- **Sparse, ranked relay support remains the strongest observed design.** The current best, Generation 5 `sparse_relay_support`, scores **0.85** with an **11.36% mean best gap** and only **264 quadratic terms**. It selects a small relay set per demanded OD pair using two-leg affinity and a relay-pressure discount, rather than rewarding every possible two-hop route. Its best gaps are **+10.0%** and **+12.8%** on the two network12 cases.
- **Combine direct-demand rewards with concave support.** The current best gives direct links negative rewards of `-(0.75*value + 0.25*sqrt(value))`; this preserves preference for high-demand direct links while assigning meaningful reward to moderate-demand links. Generation 0’s broader pressure-discounted surrogate also used linear-plus-square-root demand rewards and scored **0.83**, supporting the usefulness of this reward shape, though its **880 quadratic terms** and weaker **14.61% mean best gap** show that reward shape alone did not match sparse relay selection.
- **Discount relays by bottleneck pressure and rank paths by both legs.** Generation 5 computes node pressure from the larger of incoming and outgoing demand, floors normalized pressure at 0.65, and ranks relays by `sqrt((0.1+a)*(0.1+b)) / (0.75+pressure)`. This favors plausible, balanced two-leg routes while discounting heavily loaded nodes; the observed result is the best score and best-gap performance among the reported programs.

## Ineffective Approaches
- **Broad two-hop support produced larger models and generally weaker best gaps.** The 880-term Generation 0, Generation 4, and Generation 11 variants scored **0.83, 0.79, and 0.79**, respectively, with mean best gaps of **14.61%, 16.85%, and 18.47%**. Generation 6’s congestion/load model used **2,200** quadratic terms and scored **0.84**, but its mean best gap was **12.55%**, still above the current best’s 11.36%. These comparisons associate sparse support with better results, although the programs also differ in reward and penalty details.
- **Raising degree penalties did not reliably enforce feasibility or improve quality.** Generation 4 used penalty 5 and had **18%** mean pre-repair feasibility and a **16.85%** best gap; Generation 11 used penalty 6 and had only **3%** feasibility and an **18.47%** gap. Other earlier penalty variants (weights 4, 5, and 10) likewise scored **0.77–0.78**. The current best’s penalty is only **1.5**, but its feasibility is also low at **8%**, so these results do not show that penalty weight alone explains quality or feasibility.
- **Congestion and relay/load balancing terms did not resolve weak typical-sample performance.** Generation 6 scored **0.84** but had a **26.89% mean median gap** and **13% feasibility**; Generation 7’s congestion-aware variant scored **0.82**, with **29.21% median gap** and **10% feasibility**. Generation 6’s feedback specifically found only 1–25% valid samples despite many distinct topologies, indicating these added terms did not reliably steer samples toward strong feasible designs.

## Implementation Insights
- **The current best bounds the quadratic model per demanded OD pair.** It skips zero-demand OD pairs, chooses `min(n-2, max(2, n//4))` relays, and adds terms only for the top-ranked candidates. On the evaluated network12 instances this yielded **264 terms**, versus **880** in the broad-support variants and **2,200** in Generation 6. The smaller model coincides with the strongest score and lowest mean best gap.
- **Relay ranking uses both leg plausibility and node load.** The current code computes affinity as `sqrt((0.1+a)*(0.1+b))` and divides by `0.75 + node_pressure[j]`. Pressure is the maximum normalized incoming/outgoing demand with a 0.65 floor. This creates a concrete preference for relays with support on both legs while reducing the appeal of already-busy nodes.
- **Quadratic rewards are demand-supported, discounted, and deduplicated.** For each positive-demand `(i,k)`, the code forms a support value from the normalized OD demand, applies a relay-pressure discount to the reward, and canonicalizes the two edge keys by sorting them before dictionary insertion. The resulting **264-term** model avoids the dense 880-term construction observed in several alternatives.
- **The implementation sanitizes and normalizes demand consistently.** It clips negative traffic to zero, clears the diagonal, scales by maximum off-diagonal traffic, and protects the zero-demand case. The direct and relay rewards therefore use comparable normalized values and exclude self-link traffic.

# META RECOMMENDATIONS

The following are actionable recommendations for the next program generations:

1. **Tune the sparse relay budget with a small controlled sweep.** Keep the existing affinity-and-pressure ranking, but compare `max(1, n // 5)`, `max(2, n // 4)`, and `n // 3` relays per demanded OD pair, capped at `n - 2`. The current sparse model has the best mean gap and only 264 quadratic terms, so test nearby budgets rather than restoring broad two-hop support.

2. **Allocate extra relays selectively to high-demand OD pairs.** Start with the current relay count, then add one or two candidates only for the largest normalized demands, retaining the same ranking for choosing those candidates. This tests whether the weaker `network12_s1` result can benefit from more routing options on important flows without making the model dense for every pair.

3. **Test a more bottleneck-sensitive relay affinity.** Compare the current geometric-mean affinity with `sqrt((a + ε) * (b + ε))` using a smaller ε, and with a floored minimum-leg score such as `sqrt(min(a, b) * (max(a, b) + ε))`; keep pressure discounting and sparse selection unchanged. Requiring stronger support from both legs may reduce rewards for routes whose apparent quality is dominated by only one leg.

4. **Calibrate the pressure discount in both ranking and reward weights.** Sweep a few exponents, for example `(0.75 + pressure) ** alpha` with `alpha` around 0.75, 1.0, and 1.25, and use the same discount form for relay ranking and quadratic rewards. This isolates whether the current pressure treatment is too strong or weak while preserving a feature of the leading design.

5. **Tune the direct and two-hop reward balance independently.** Keep the direct reward’s linear-plus-square-root form, but test modest changes to its mixture and to the quadratic multiplier around the current `0.75/0.25` and `0.25` settings, changing one at a time. The current blend performs well alongside sparse support, while penalty increases have not reliably improved results; avoid changing the degree penalty in the same experiment.
