# GLOBAL INSIGHTS SCRATCHPAD

The following are global insights about optimization approaches and their effectiveness:

## Successful Algorithmic Patterns
- **The current best, Generation 6’s *Demand-Aware Surrogate QUBO Terms*, remains the aggregate leader.** It scores **0.82** with a **12.64% mean best-solution gap** across six instances. Its direct-demand link rewards combined with modest endpoint-exposure penalties are still the strongest overall pattern among the programs compared.
- **Targeted sender/receiver conflict penalties outperform the evaluated relay-heavy alternatives in aggregate.** Generation 6 uses **1,320** quadratic terms and achieves a **12.64%** mean gap. The new relay-oriented Generation 21 (*Demand-Weighted Relay Surrogate QUBO*) reaches **0.82 / 12.68%**, nearly tying it, while Generation 22’s two-hop design is **0.79 / 16.02%** and Generation 23’s balanced two-hop design is **0.78 / 17.95%**.
- **Direct-demand signals with endpoint balancing remain competitive without path-reward complexity.** Generation 24’s *Concave Demand Endpoint-Balancing QUBO* scores **0.82 / 13.47%** with about **541** quadratic terms. Its result is close to Generation 6, though it has a **0.83 percentage-point** larger mean gap and only **6%** average pre-repair feasibility.
- **Generation 6’s endpoint-exposure formulation performs well across all six instances.** Its scores range from **0.798** on `sub12_s5` to **0.865** on `sub12_s4`, with best-solution gaps of **9.76–15.50%**. In particular, its `sub12_s4` result is stronger than the new programs’ reported weakest-instance results, although Generation 24 also does well on that instance.

## Ineffective Approaches
- **More elaborate two-hop rewards did not beat the current best and sometimes widened the gap substantially.** Generation 22 scores **0.79 / 16.02%**, and Generation 23 scores **0.78 / 17.95%**; Generation 23’s largest gap is **28.7%** on `sub12_s2`. These results are weaker than Generation 6’s **0.82 / 12.64%**.
- **Adding relay rewards has not shown a consistent aggregate benefit.** Generation 21’s relay surrogate is close at **0.82 / 12.68%**, but its average median gap is **32.76%** and only **0–8%** of samples are valid before repair. Earlier relay variants in the previous evaluations also trailed Generation 6, generally scoring **0.79–0.80** with mean gaps of **15.60–17.64%**.
- **Low pre-repair feasibility persists across different objective designs.** Generation 6 averages **18%**, but drops to **5%** on `sub12_s3` and `sub12_s5`. The new programs report similarly weak averages: Generation 21 has **0–8%**, Generation 23 **16%**, and Generation 24 **6%**. Thus, objective changes have not reliably made sampled topologies constraint-feasible.
- **Strong best samples mask poor typical outcomes.** Generation 6’s mean median gap is **31.82%**, including **41.2%** on `sub12_s5`; Generation 24 averages **32.95%**, and Generation 23 **42.74%**. The best-sample metric therefore does not capture the substantial quality variation across QAOA samples.

## Implementation Insights
- **Generation 6 rewards direct service and penalizes concentrated endpoint exposure.** It sets each linear coefficient to `-direct / max_demand`. Its exposure estimate is `direct + 0.10 * (row[i] + col[j]) / (n - 1)`, and it applies positive pairwise penalties to links sharing a sender or receiver, scaled by `0.18 / max_demand`. This design yields **0.82 / 12.64%** with **1,320** quadratic terms.
- **The current best uses a modest, explicit estimate of endpoint pressure rather than rewarding indirect routes.** The pressure term is only **10%** of endpoint row/column traffic, and the pair coefficient is **0.18** before demand normalization. This is consistent with its better aggregate result than the more path-focused Generation 22 (**0.79 / 16.02%**) and Generation 23 (**0.78 / 17.95%**).
- **Fixed degree-penalty weights alone do not explain performance differences.** Generation 6 uses **4.0**, while Generation 21 uses **1.0**, Generation 22 **4.0**, Generation 23 **2.5**, and Generation 24 **2.0**. Despite that range, feasibility remains low for most programs, and Generation 6’s advantage is better characterized by its coefficient structure than by penalty weight alone.
- **Quadratic-term count is not a reliable proxy for quality.** Generation 24 reaches **13.47%** mean gap with about **541** terms; Generation 21 uses **810–880** terms and reaches **12.68%**; Generation 6 uses **1,320** terms and reaches **12.64%**. By contrast, the two-hop Generation 23 has about **867** terms and a **17.95%** gap. The evaluated evidence points to the interaction design and scaling, rather than term count itself, as the meaningful difference.

## Performance Analysis
- **Generation 6 remains the best by mean gap among the current and newly evaluated programs.** Its **12.64%** gap narrowly beats Generation 21’s **12.68%**, and is better than Generation 24’s **13.47%**, Generation 22’s **16.02%**, and Generation 23’s **17.95%**. Its combined score is **0.82**, tied at the reported precision with Generations 21 and 24.
- **The latest results reinforce a gap between balanced direct-link designs and more complex path-based objectives.** Generation 24’s concave direct rewards and endpoint penalties are close to the leader at **13.47%**; Generation 22 and Generation 23, which add two-hop route rewards, have larger gaps of **16.02%** and **17.95%**, respectively.
- **Generation 6’s strongest instance is `sub12_s4`, while its most persistent weaknesses are sample quality and feasibility on `sub12_s5` and `sub12_s3`.** It reaches **0.865 / 9.76%** on `sub12_s4`, but has only **5%** valid samples on each of `sub12_s3` and `sub12_s5`; `sub12_s5` also has its worst median gap, **41.2%**.
- **Across the evaluations, best-solution quality and typical-sample quality remain distinct.** Generation 6’s best gaps average **12.64%**, but its median gaps average **31.82%**. The new programs show the same pattern, with median-gap averages of **32.76%** for Generation 21, **40.04%** for Generation 22, **42.74%** for Generation 23, and **32.95%** for Generation 24.

# META RECOMMENDATIONS

The following are actionable recommendations for the next program generations:

1. **Sweep the endpoint-conflict scale while keeping the current objective fixed.** Test `pair_scale = c / scale` for `c` around the successful `0.18`, such as `0.10`, `0.14`, and `0.24`. This isolates whether stronger or weaker same-sender and same-receiver penalties can improve the weak-feasibility cases without adding broader interactions.

2. **Tune endpoint pressure independently.** Compare the current `0.10` multiplier in `pressure` with values such as `0.05` and `0.20`, leaving the direct rewards and pair construction unchanged. The modest exposure estimate is part of the best-performing design, so a focused sweep can test whether it better captures endpoint load on the weaker `sub12_s3` and `sub12_s5` instances.

3. **Scale conflict penalties by the shared endpoint’s normalized traffic.** For links sharing a sender, weight their conflict using that sender’s normalized row demand; for links sharing a receiver, use the corresponding column demand. Keep the current exposure-product penalty as a baseline and bound the load multiplier to avoid letting a few high-traffic endpoints dominate.

4. **Test a concave direct-demand reward while retaining the successful conflict structure.** Replace `-direct / scale` in the linear term with a normalized transform such as `-sqrt(direct / scale)`, leaving exposure and pair penalties unchanged. This tests whether reducing the dominance of the largest demands helps while preserving the targeted endpoint interactions that distinguish the current best.

5. **Add only a small, normalized two-hop reward to the existing direct-demand objective.** Allocate each two-hop demand contribution across eligible relays rather than crediting every path in full, and keep its total weight modest relative to direct rewards. Direct-link designs outperform the more elaborate two-hop alternatives, so this tests whether limited indirect credit complements—rather than replaces—the current successful objective.
