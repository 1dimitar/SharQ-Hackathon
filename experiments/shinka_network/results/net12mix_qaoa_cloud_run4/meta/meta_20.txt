# GLOBAL INSIGHTS SCRATCHPAD

The following are global insights about optimization approaches and their effectiveness:

## Successful Algorithmic Patterns
- **The current best, Generation 6’s *Demand-Aware Surrogate QUBO Terms*, remains the strongest aggregate result.** It scores **0.82** with a **12.64% mean best-solution gap**, outperforming all five newly evaluated programs, whose scores range from **0.79–0.81** and mean gaps from **14.85–17.64%**. Its direct-demand rewards paired with endpoint-exposure conflict penalties remain the clearest successful pattern.
- **Targeted penalties for links sharing a sender or receiver are consistently more effective here than the newer relay-heavy variants.** Generation 6 uses **1,320** quadratic terms and achieves a **12.64%** gap. By comparison, Generation 17’s balanced two-hop rewards and congestion penalties use roughly **2,130–2,200** terms but reach a **15.85%** mean gap; Generation 16’s relay-pressure design reaches **15.60%**.
- **Generation 6’s advantage holds across the highlighted instances, not just in aggregate.** It scores **0.844** on `network12_s0` with an **11.29%** gap, and **0.865** on `sub12_s4` with a **9.76%** gap. Generation 15 also does best on `sub12_s4`, but its score is **0.90** with a **7.50%** gap according to its summary—an instance-level exception that does not change its weaker aggregate result of **0.81 / 14.85%**.

## Ineffective Approaches
- **Adding relay rewards has not improved on the current best in these evaluations.** Generation 16, which allocates two-hop rewards by inverse node load, scores **0.80 / 15.60%**; Generation 17’s balanced relay rewards score **0.80 / 15.85%**; Generation 18’s selective relay bonuses score **0.79 / 16.47%**; and Generation 20’s concave direct rewards plus relay allocation score **0.79 / 17.64%**. Each trails Generation 6’s **0.82 / 12.64%**.
- **More quadratic interactions have not translated into better solution quality.** Generation 17 uses roughly **2,130–2,200** terms and reports only **2–42%** feasibility before repair, while Generation 6 uses **1,320** terms and averages **18%** feasibility. The newer relay programs therefore add structural interactions without a corresponding aggregate gain.
- **Low pre-repair feasibility remains a shared weakness, and fixed degree penalties do not resolve it.** Generation 6 averages **18%** feasibility, but falls to **5%** on `sub12_s3` and `sub12_s5`. The new programs also show low rates: Generation 15 averages **13%**, Generation 16 **10%**, Generation 18 **6%**, and Generation 20 **11%**. Several use fixed penalty weights from **1.5 to 4.0**, with no consistent feasibility improvement.
- **Best-sample scores conceal weaker typical outcomes.** Generation 6’s mean median gap is **31.82%**, with a **41.2%** median gap on `sub12_s5`; other relay variants also report high median gaps, including **36.59%** for Generation 16 and **28.7–47.2%** for Generation 20. Strong best samples do not imply that most sampled topologies are competitive.

## Implementation Insights
- **Generation 6 combines direct service rewards with a modest, targeted endpoint-conflict signal.** It sets each linear weight to `-direct / scale`, where `scale` is maximum demand. For each link it estimates exposure as direct demand plus `0.10 * (row[i] + col[j]) / (n - 1)`, then penalizes pairs sharing a sender or receiver with weight `0.18 / scale * exposure[e1] * exposure[e2] / scale`. This design scores **0.82 / 12.64%** with **1,320** quadratic terms.
- **The best program’s endpoint signal appears more useful than simply broadening the objective with path rewards.** Generation 15 also uses direct-link rewards and endpoint-sharing penalties, scoring **0.81 / 14.85%**. Its summary reports a penalty weight of **3.5** and low feasibility (**13%**), versus Generation 6’s penalty weight of **4.0** and **18%** mean feasibility. The shared structure is competitive, but Generation 6’s particular exposure scaling and coefficients perform better overall.
- **Relay variants differ substantially in allocation, but none matches the current best.** Generation 16 distributes two-hop rewards according to inverse node load and uses a degree penalty of **1.5**; Generation 17 balances incoming and outgoing relay traffic and uses **3.0**; Generation 18 limits bonuses to preferred relays and uses **4.0**; Generation 20 reduces relay quality for high-load nodes and uses **1.5**. Their scores span **0.79–0.80**, with mean gaps of **15.60–17.64%**.
- **Term count alone is not predictive of performance.** Generation 6’s **1,320** terms outperform Generation 17’s approximately **2,130–2,200** and Generation 18’s reported **many distinct topologies** with only **6%** feasibility. The results align with earlier comparisons: Generation 7’s roughly **2,160** terms reached a **15.01%** gap, while Generation 10’s roughly **336** terms reached **13.92%**. The quality and scaling of couplings matter more than their number.

## Performance Analysis
- **Generation 6 remains the leader across the evaluated programs.** Its **0.82** score and **12.64%** mean gap beat Generation 15 (**0.81 / 14.85%**), Generation 16 (**0.80 / 15.60%**), Generation 17 (**0.80 / 15.85%**), Generation 18 (**0.79 / 16.47%**), and Generation 20 (**0.79 / 17.64%**). Generation 15 is the closest by mean gap, but remains **2.21 percentage points** behind.
- **The current best’s instance results show both a strong case and persistent weak spots.** Its best highlighted result is `sub12_s4` (**0.865**, **9.76%** gap); its largest gap is `sub12_s3` (**15.50%**), where only **5%** of samples are feasible. `sub12_s5` has a smaller best gap (**12.62%**) but the worst median gap (**41.2%**) and also only **5%** feasibility.
- **The new programs have isolated instance wins but do not show a consistent aggregate advantage.** Generation 15 reports its best outcome on `sub12_s4` (**0.90**, **7.50%** gap), better than Generation 6’s result there, but its overall mean gap is **14.85%** versus **12.64%**. Generation 18 also identifies `sub12_s4` as its best instance, while `sub12_s5` is its weakest at a **24.5%** gap.
- **Across generations, relay complexity and fixed penalty choices do not correlate with better scores or feasibility.** The relay-based programs cluster around **0.79–0.80**, below Generation 6’s **0.82**, while their feasibility rates remain mostly in the **6–11%** range (with Generation 17 reporting **2–42%** by instance). Generation 6 is still imperfect, but its combination of lower term count, best aggregate gap, and higher mean feasibility is the strongest overall result.

# META RECOMMENDATIONS

The following are actionable recommendations for the next program generations:

1. **Sweep the endpoint-conflict scale while keeping the current objective otherwise unchanged.** Test `pair_scale = c / scale` for `c` values such as `0.10`, `0.14`, and `0.24` around the successful `0.18`. This directly probes whether stronger or weaker same-sender/same-receiver penalties can improve the weak-feasibility cases without adding the broader interactions that have underperformed.

2. **Tune the traffic-pressure component of exposure independently.** Compare the current coefficient `0.10` with values such as `0.05` and `0.20`, leaving direct rewards and pair construction fixed. The existing direct-demand rewards plus targeted endpoint penalties are the strongest observed pattern, so this tests whether better endpoint-load estimates can improve those couplings without changing the objective’s structure.

3. **Make conflict penalties depend on the shared endpoint’s normalized traffic load.** For same-sender pairs, scale the penalty by a bounded measure of that sender’s row demand; for same-receiver pairs, use the corresponding column demand. Keep the current exposure product and global scale as a baseline, and compare against it to test whether concentrating penalties on pressured endpoints helps—particularly on `sub12_s3` and `sub12_s5`, where pre-repair feasibility is only 5%.

4. **Try a modest, normalized two-hop reward as an addition to—not a replacement for—the direct-demand objective.** Allocate each two-hop demand contribution across eligible relays so that it is not counted in full for every path, and keep its total contribution small relative to the direct rewards. Relay-heavy objectives have trailed the best program, so this tests only whether restrained indirect credit complements its successful endpoint-conflict structure.

5. **Test a concave direct reward while preserving the current conflict penalties.** Replace `direct / scale` in the linear term with a normalized transform such as `sqrt(direct / scale)`, then keep the quadratic construction and coefficient unchanged for a clean comparison. This checks whether reducing dominance by the largest demands helps, while retaining the targeted endpoint interactions that distinguish the current best.
