# GLOBAL INSIGHTS SCRATCHPAD

The following are global insights about optimization approaches and their effectiveness:

## Successful Algorithmic Patterns
- **Balanced endpoint-load penalties improved the mean best-gap while preserving a competitive score.** Generation 6, *Demand-Weighted Surrogate QUBO*, penalizes pairs of links sharing a sender or receiver, weighted by endpoint exposure. It scored **0.82** with a **12.64% mean best-gap**, improving on Generation 7’s endpoint-load variant (**0.80**, **15.01% gap**) and Generation 10’s sparse two-hop design (**0.81**, **13.92% gap**). This matches the earlier finding that incorporating structural information beyond direct demand can help.
- **The current best’s relay-aware allocation remains the strongest established pattern.** Generation 1 combines normalized direct-demand rewards with two-hop rewards distributed among suitable relays, using balanced in/out traffic and endpoint affinity. It scored **0.82** with a **13.44% mean best-gap** and delivered strong sub-12 results, including **0.85** on `sub12_s4`. Across the evaluated programs, its structured allocation of indirect reward remains a more reliable path-based result than variants that add broader or differently scaled couplings.
- **The current best Generation 6 is strongest on several individual instances.** Its endpoint-exposure formulation achieved **0.844** on `network12_s0` and **0.865** on `sub12_s4`, with best-gaps of **11.29%** and **9.76%** respectively. `sub12_s4` was also the best result among Generation 6’s six instances, consistent with demand-informed link scoring working well on that instance.

## Ineffective Approaches
- **Adding broad endpoint and path interactions did not consistently improve over more selective path allocation.** Generation 7’s endpoint-load and two-hop model scored **0.80** with a **15.01% gap** and generated about **2,160 quadratic terms**; Generation 10’s sparse relay model scored **0.81** with a **13.92% gap**. Both underperformed Generation 6’s **0.82 / 12.64%**, while earlier two-hop variants likewise trailed Generation 1’s relay-aware allocation.
- **The current best’s best-sample quality masks inconsistent results.** Generation 6’s mean best-gap is **12.64%**, but its mean median gap is **31.82%**. In particular, `sub12_s5` has a **41.2% median gap** and only **5%** pre-repair feasibility; `sub12_s3` has the largest best-gap (**15.5%**) and also only **5%** feasibility. Strong best samples therefore do not indicate consistently good or valid QAOA outputs.
- **Fixed degree-penalty weights have not resolved feasibility.** Generation 6 uses a fixed weight of **4.0**, but only **18%** of samples were feasible on average, ranging from **5%** on `sub12_s3` and `sub12_s5` to **40%** on `sub12_s1`. Other variants with fixed weights from **1.0 to 3.0** also reported very low feasibility, so the reported results do not show a simple relationship between a larger fixed penalty and reliable validity.

## Implementation Insights
- **Generation 6 combines a simple direct-service signal with explicit load competition.** Its linear coefficient is `-direct / scale`; quadratic terms pair links sharing a sender or receiver and weight them by the product of endpoint exposures. Exposure combines direct demand with a modest row/column traffic-pressure estimate. This produced **1,320 quadratic terms per instance** and the best mean gap among the new evaluations.
- **Scaling and signs matter to the intended objective.** Generation 6 normalizes direct rewards by maximum demand and scales its positive quadratic congestion terms by `0.18 / scale`, with a further division by `scale` in the exposure product. The linear terms reward direct traffic, while positive pair terms discourage concentrating estimated exposure at shared endpoints.
- **Selective interaction structure appears more effective than term count alone.** Generation 6’s **1,320** terms accompany a **12.64%** mean gap, while Generation 7’s roughly **2,160** terms accompany a **15.01%** gap and Generation 10’s **336** terms accompany a **13.92%** gap. As earlier comparisons also showed for Generation 1’s roughly **840** terms, the number of terms by itself does not explain performance; the interaction design and reward allocation distinguish outcomes.
- **The earlier best’s relay scoring complements the direct-demand baseline differently from endpoint-load penalties.** Generation 1 distributes indirect reward across candidate relays using balanced incoming/outgoing traffic and pair suitability, rather than penalizing every same-sender or same-receiver pair. Its **13.44%** mean gap and strong sub-12 scores remain evidence for the value of carefully allocated path credit, while Generation 6 shows that endpoint exposure can also produce a competitive result.

## Performance Analysis
- **Generation 6 leads the new comparisons by mean best-gap, while matching the top combined score.** It scored **0.82** with a **12.64%** gap, compared with Generation 5’s **0.81 / 14.14%**, Generation 7’s **0.80 / 15.01%**, Generation 8’s **0.82 / 12.64%**, and Generation 10’s **0.81 / 13.92%**. Its gap is also slightly lower than the earlier Generation 1 result of **13.44%**, although both have a combined score of **0.82**.
- **Generation 6’s instance results are uneven despite the best overall gap.** Best-gaps range from **9.76%** on `sub12_s4` to **15.50%** on `sub12_s3`; scores range from **0.798** on `sub12_s5` to **0.865** on `sub12_s4`. The largest gap is specifically `sub12_s3`, whereas Generation 5’s weakest case was `sub12_s1` at **20.8%**, and Generation 10’s weakest was also `sub12_s1` at **22.6%**.
- **The mean best-gap improved relative to several variants, but feasibility and median quality remain weak.** Generation 6’s **12.64%** mean best-gap is better than Generation 5’s **14.14%**, Generation 7’s **15.01%**, and Generation 10’s **13.92%**. However, its **18%** mean feasible rate and **31.82%** mean median gap show that this improvement chiefly reflects best samples, not uniformly strong QAOA outcomes.
- **Results vary with instance and do not track quadratic-term count monotonically.** Generation 6 obtains its best gap on `sub12_s4` despite using the same **1,320** terms on every instance, and its weakest feasibility occurs on `sub12_s3` and `sub12_s5`. Across programs, both denser endpoint/path models and the sparse Generation 10 model trail it, reinforcing that term count alone is not a predictor of score.

# META RECOMMENDATIONS

The following are actionable recommendations for the next program generations:

1. **Tune endpoint-exposure congestion strength while preserving its current structure.** Sweep the `0.18` pair scale over a modest range, such as `0.10`, `0.14`, and `0.24`, keeping the direct-demand rewards and exposure calculation fixed. Generation 6’s selective same-sender/same-receiver penalties produced the best mean gap, but its low feasibility suggests testing whether slightly weaker congestion terms improve the balance between useful links and endpoint spreading.

2. **Make endpoint exposure more demand-sensitive without broadening the interaction graph.** Compare the current `direct + 0.10 * pressure` exposure with a few nearby pressure weights, such as `0.05` and `0.20`, or apply a concave transform to the normalized exposure before forming pair products. This directly tests the successful demand-weighted endpoint pattern while limiting changes to the already effective set of 1,320 pair terms.

3. **Combine endpoint-load penalties with selective relay credit.** Add a small, normalized linear reward for links that serve as useful relays, distributing each OD pair’s two-hop reward across eligible relays rather than duplicating it. Generation 1’s relay-aware allocation was a strong independent pattern, and testing a modest contribution alongside Generation 6’s congestion penalties could capture path value without adopting the broader interactions that underperformed.

4. **Adapt congestion penalties to endpoint load instead of applying one global pair scale.** Scale each same-sender or same-receiver pair penalty using a normalized estimate of that node’s traffic pressure, while keeping penalties bounded and the interaction structure unchanged. This may target overloaded endpoints more effectively; it is motivated by Generation 6’s exposure-based success and could address the especially poor feasibility on `sub12_s3` and `sub12_s5`.

5. **Reduce dominance by the largest demands in the direct reward.** Test replacing `direct / scale` with a concave normalized reward such as `sqrt(direct / scale)`, while retaining the current exposure-based pair penalties and separately normalizing the two components to comparable magnitudes. This probes whether smaller demands deserve more influence across topologies, without discarding the proven direct-service signal or the best program’s selective endpoint competition.
