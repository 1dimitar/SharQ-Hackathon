# GLOBAL INSIGHTS SCRATCHPAD

The following are global insights about optimization approaches and their effectiveness:

## Successful Algorithmic Patterns
- **The current best, Generation 6’s endpoint-exposure QUBO, remains the strongest result overall.** It scores **0.82** with a **12.64% mean best-gap**, matching the top combined score while beating the newly evaluated generations’ mean gaps of **15.57–22.06%**. Its direct-demand link rewards are complemented by positive pairwise penalties for links sharing a sender or receiver, discouraging concentrated endpoint exposure.
- **Balanced relay allocation remains a competitive alternative, but did not surpass the current best.** Generation 12, *Demand-Weighted Relay QUBO Surrogate*, scores **0.81** with a **15.57% gap**. Its indirect reward is distributed among relays using balanced incoming/outgoing traffic and endpoint suitability. Earlier evidence remains consistent: Generation 1’s similar relay-aware allocation scored **0.82** with a **13.44% gap**, close to Generation 6.
- **A useful structural signal need not come from dense path rewards.** Generation 13’s sparse relay diversification scores **0.77** with a **19.20% gap**, while Generation 14’s concave direct rewards and relay scoring reach **0.79** with a **17.49% gap**. These results are below Generation 6, but both outperform the weakest two-hop variants, suggesting that selective indirect credit can help relative to simple demand-only rewards.
- **Generation 6’s instance-level results demonstrate where endpoint-exposure scoring is especially effective.** It achieves **0.844** on `network12_s0` (an **11.29% gap**) and **0.865** on `sub12_s4` (a **9.76% gap**), the strongest highlighted outcomes in its evaluation.

## Ineffective Approaches
- **The newly evaluated two-hop variants did not beat the endpoint-exposure best.** Generations 9 and 11 each score **0.75** (gaps **22.06%** and **19.86%**); Generation 13 scores **0.77 / 19.20%**; and Generation 14 scores **0.79 / 17.49%**. Their path-reward designs therefore underperform Generation 6’s **0.82 / 12.64%** on aggregate.
- **Fixed degree penalties and surrogate rewards do not reliably produce feasible QAOA samples.** The new variants report mean feasibility of only **3–9%**, while Generation 6 reaches **18%**—still modest, but higher. Even Generation 6’s fixed penalty of **4.0** has only **5%** feasibility on `sub12_s3` and `sub12_s5`; the results do not show that simply increasing a fixed penalty solves validity.
- **Broad or differently scaled interactions remain unreliable.** Earlier Generation 7’s endpoint-load/two-hop design used roughly **2,160 quadratic terms** and scored **0.80 / 15.01% gap**; the newer path models also trail Generation 6 despite using hundreds of terms. The pattern reinforces that interaction design and coefficient allocation matter more than simply adding path rewards or terms.
- **Best-sample quality can obscure poor typical outcomes.** Generation 6’s mean best-gap is **12.64%**, but its mean median gap is **31.82%**. The median gap reaches **41.2%** on `sub12_s5`, despite a **12.62%** best-gap there, and pre-repair feasibility is only **5%** on that instance.

## Implementation Insights
- **Generation 6 uses a targeted endpoint-conflict construction.** It sets each linear coefficient to `-direct / scale`, where `scale` is maximum demand, then computes endpoint exposure from direct demand plus `0.10 * (row[i] + col[j]) / (n - 1)`. Positive quadratic terms penalize pairs sharing a sender or receiver, with weight `0.18 / scale * exposure[e1] * exposure[e2] / scale`. This combines direct-service rewards with a modest structural load signal.
- **The current best’s selective coupling is consistent with better aggregate gaps than several path-heavy alternatives.** Generation 6 has **1,320** quadratic terms and a **12.64%** mean gap. Generation 7’s roughly **2,160** terms yielded **15.01%**, while Generation 10’s roughly **336** terms yielded **13.92%**. Term count alone does not explain the result; the endpoint-conflict signal appears more consequential than density.
- **Relay-based programs differ in how they allocate indirect credit.** Generation 12 distributes bounded two-hop rewards across relays according to balanced in/out traffic and endpoint suitability, scoring **0.81 / 15.57%**. Generation 11 uses inverse traffic pressure and a fixed penalty of **4.0**, scoring **0.75 / 19.86%**. These results support relay-aware allocation as a viable pattern, but show that the choice of relay weighting and overall objective balance materially affects performance.
- **Concave direct rewards and sparse relay terms trade performance for altered demand sensitivity, without improving on the best.** Generation 14 compresses direct-demand differences with negative square-root weights and uses a **0.30** two-hop discount; it scores **0.79 / 17.49%** with only **3%** feasibility. Generation 13 uses sparse rewards for up to two suitable relays and a **1.25** penalty, scoring **0.77 / 19.20%** with **9%** feasibility.

## Performance Analysis
- **Generation 6 remains the aggregate leader against the five new programs.** Its **0.82** combined score and **12.64%** mean best-gap exceed Generation 9 (**0.75 / 22.06%**), Generation 11 (**0.75 / 19.86%**), Generation 12 (**0.81 / 15.57%**), Generation 13 (**0.77 / 19.20%**), and Generation 14 (**0.79 / 17.49%**). Generation 12 is the closest new result, but has a gap nearly three percentage points larger.
- **The current best is strong on both network and sub-12 cases, but its weakest instances remain identifiable.** It scores **0.844** on `network12_s0` and **0.865** on `sub12_s4`, but its largest gap is `sub12_s3` at **15.50%**, where only **5%** of samples are feasible. `sub12_s5` has a smaller best-gap (**12.62%**) but the worst median gap (**41.2%**) and likewise only **5%** feasibility.
- **The new programs show notable instance sensitivity rather than a consistent winner over the current best.** Generation 13 is particularly strong on `sub12_s4` (**0.834**, **5.8% gap**) but has a **34.1%** gap on `network12_s0`. Generation 9’s `sub12_s4` result is much weaker (**0.46**, **58.6% gap**), while Generation 14’s best highlighted case is also `sub12_s4` (**0.839**, **11.51% gap**). The current best’s `sub12_s4` result remains higher at **0.865**.
- **Feasibility remains a limitation even for the top-scoring formulation.** Generation 6 averages **18%** pre-repair feasibility, versus **3–9%** for the newly evaluated programs, but its `sub12_s3` and `sub12_s5` feasibility rates are only **5%**. Thus its best-sample advantage is real, but the median-gap and feasibility data show that it does not consistently guide QAOA to good valid topologies.

# META RECOMMENDATIONS

The following are actionable recommendations for the next program generations:

1. **Tune the endpoint-conflict strength while keeping the current interaction structure fixed.** Sweep the pair scale `0.18` over values such as `0.10`, `0.14`, and `0.24`, retaining the direct rewards and exposure calculation. The existing same-sender/same-receiver penalties are the strongest observed pattern, but the low feasibility on `sub12_s3` and `sub12_s5` makes their strength worth testing.

2. **Vary the traffic-pressure contribution to endpoint exposure.** Compare the current `0.10 * (row[i] + col[j]) / (n - 1)` term with smaller and larger coefficients, such as `0.05` and `0.20`, while leaving direct rewards and pair construction unchanged. This tests whether better endpoint-load estimates can improve the current best’s congestion signal without adding broad path interactions.

3. **Add a small, bounded relay reward to the existing endpoint-exposure objective.** Distribute each two-hop demand contribution across eligible relays, rather than awarding the full contribution to every possible path, and normalize it so it remains modest relative to direct rewards. Relay-aware allocation has performed competitively on its own, so this tests whether selective indirect credit complements the current best’s endpoint penalties.

4. **Make endpoint-conflict penalties sensitive to the shared node’s traffic pressure.** Scale each same-sender or same-receiver pair penalty by a bounded, normalized measure of that node’s incoming or outgoing demand, instead of using one global pair scale. This preserves the proven selective coupling pattern while testing whether congestion penalties can better target overloaded endpoints.

5. **Test a concave direct-demand reward while retaining endpoint-exposure penalties.** Replace `direct / scale` in the linear reward with a normalized transform such as `sqrt(direct / scale)`, and normalize the linear and quadratic components to comparable magnitudes. Concave direct rewards may reduce dominance by the largest demands; unlike the weaker concave variant already tested, this keeps the current best’s successful endpoint-conflict structure.
