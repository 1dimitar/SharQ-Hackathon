# INDIVIDUAL PROGRAM SUMMARIES

The following are summaries of individual programs evaluated since the last meta update:

**Program Name: Pressure-Discounted Two-Hop QUBO Surrogate**
- **Implementation**: Normalizes nonnegative, non-self demand and assigns negative linear weights using a demand/square-root blend. For each positive-demand pair, it selects a small set of two-hop routes by pressure-adjusted affinity, adds a low-pressure alternative, and returns a fixed penalty weight of 1.25.
- **Performance**: Combined score is 0.89 (raw 0.83, post-processed 0.96); mean best and median gaps are 11.66% and 32.42%, with 91% feasibility and 383 quadratic terms on average.
- **Feedback**: The surrogate builds quickly and produces consistently strong post-processed results, reducing the mean gap to 4.38%. Before repair, median gaps remain around 29–37%, and the largest best-sample gap is 16.8% for network14_s0, so its strongest contribution is as a head start for the fixed post-processor.

- **Sparse, demand-supported two-hop routes are consistently competitive.** The current best ranks intermediates using endpoint affinity, leg evidence, and transit pressure; it uses a harmonic-mean bilateral-support factor so a strong leg cannot compensate for a weak one. Generation 99’s transit-pressure design scores **0.903** with **403.83** quadratic terms and a **3.78%** repaired gap. Generation 97 also uses sparse top-ranked routes and scores **0.90**, though its repaired gap is **4.22%**. These results support selective route incentives rather than rewarding every possible transit node.

- **Demand-aware small-cut terms are associated with strong repaired results.** The current best approximates two-node boundary capacity with an inverse-square correction and reports a **3.97%** post-processed gap. Related cut-aware designs—Generation 94 at **0.90 / 3.97%**, and Generation 99 at **0.903 / 3.78%**—are similarly strong. The evidence supports cut-load information as a useful component, though the models differ in other respects.

- **Demand normalization and direct-link rewards provide a stable foundation.** The current best sanitizes and clips demand, normalizes before computing shares, and combines \(z^{0.78}\), \(\sqrt z\), and bilateral-share evidence. Its sample-quality scores stay within **0.840–0.855** across sizes \(n=10\) to \(16\), rather than degrading monotonically with network size.

## Ineffective Approaches

- **The Generation 96 builder failed validation and yielded no algorithmic performance evidence.** Its route-factor code referenced `self.row_share` and `self.col_share` in a context where `self` was undefined, resulting in a **0.0** score. This is an implementation failure, not evidence against the intended demand-aware route method.

- **High feasibility and topology diversity alone do not ensure strong raw samples.** The current best has **93%** mean feasibility and 64 distinct topologies per case, yet its mean best-sample gap is **10.56%** and median gap is **27.01%**. Generation 97 similarly reports **95%** feasibility but a **10.87%** mean best gap. These results show that validity and diversity do not eliminate substantial objective gaps before repair.

- **Post-processing can conceal weak pre-repair sample quality.** The current best’s gap improves from a **10.56%** mean best-sample gap to **3.97%** after repair; Generation 99 reports raw gaps of **8.0–15.4%** but a **3.78%** repaired gap. The large median raw gaps (about **23–31%** across these designs) indicate that strong head-start scores do not imply that most sampled topologies are already near-optimal.

## Implementation Insights

- **The current best combines several mechanisms while keeping route construction selective.** For each positive OD pair it ranks transit nodes using endpoint affinity, edge evidence, and pressure, retains two routes, and allows a third only for substantial demand when the third candidate is close in rank. It then scales route reward by demand support, route rank, affinity, and transit pressure. This produces **699.83 mean quadratic terms**, between Generation 99’s **403.83** and the more route/pair-heavy Generation 98’s roughly **889–3,808**.

- **Bilateral leg support limits misleading route incentives.** The current best uses \(2ab/(a+b)\) for route-leg support, where \(a\) and \(b\) are the two leg-demand signals. It also adds positive direct-route redundancy corrections, reducing the incentive for an indirect route when the direct link already carries demand. This is a more selective form of route support than simply rewarding a path based on aggregate endpoint affinity.

- **The cut approximation is translated into linear and quadratic terms with valid-topology offsets removed.** For binary reciprocal links, the implementation expands \(1/(4-x-y)^2\) into constant, linear, and pair terms, weights it by squared external demand, then removes linear offsets constant over valid degree-two topologies. This avoids unnecessary competition with the evaluator’s degree constraints while retaining the cut signal.

- **Demand sanitation and scale-aware construction support robust evaluation.** The current best replaces non-finite values, clips negative demand, clears the diagonal, and normalizes before summing, avoiding overflow and making rewards insensitive to overall demand scale. It also returns early when there is no positive demand and uses a modest size-dependent penalty weight. Construction remains fast (generally **0.00–0.04 seconds**, with one reported **0.27-second** case).

## Performance Analysis

- **The current best is competitive with, but not clearly ahead of, the strongest recent variants.** Its reported detailed metrics are **0.8461 sample quality**, **0.9620 head-start**, and **3.97%** mean repaired gap; the summary rounds the combined score to **0.90**. Generation 99 reports **0.903 combined** and **3.78%** gap, while Generation 95 reports **0.90** and **3.76%**. These are close results, not evidence of a large performance separation.

- **The current best’s repaired performance is consistently useful across sizes, while its raw gaps remain substantial.** Per-case post-processed gaps range from **0.47%** (network10_s0) to **6.67%** (network13_s2); raw best gaps range from **7.4%** to **13.4%**, with the largest on network16_s0. The mean feasibility rate falls to **81%** on that largest case, although the per-size sample-quality score remains around **0.849** for \(n=16\).

- **Lower quadratic-term count does not alone predict better results.** Generation 99 uses about **404** terms and achieves **0.903 / 3.78%**; the current best uses about **700** and achieves **0.90 / 3.97%**; Generation 97 uses about **744** and reports **0.90 / 4.22%**. These measurements support compactness as a useful property, but indicate that term selection and represented structure matter more than term count in isolation.

- **The evaluated designs share a strong repair-driven profile.** The current best’s mean post score is **0.96** against a mean raw score of **0.85**. Generation 99 similarly reports a **0.842** sample-quality component and **0.964** head-start, while Generation 97’s post-processing reduces its mean gap from **10.87%** to **4.22%**. Across these programs, repaired quality is consistently stronger than the quality of the sampled topologies before repair.

# META RECOMMENDATIONS

The following are actionable recommendations for the next program generations:

1. **Tighten third-route admission with a controlled threshold sweep.** Keep the two-route default and existing high-demand cutoff, but compare rank-ratio thresholds around 0.95–0.98 against the current 0.93. The current best’s sparse routes are competitive, so admitting a third route only when candidates are especially close may reduce diffuse incentives and improve raw sample quality without a broad increase in quadratic terms.

2. **Add a modest congestion discount to route incentives.** Estimate each intermediary’s load from the demand support of the routes assigned to it, then mildly discount incentives through heavily loaded intermediaries. Preserve the harmonic-mean bilateral leg factor so congestion balancing does not make a weakly supported route attractive; transit-pressure designs have achieved competitive repaired gaps.

3. **Refine route support with endpoint shares while retaining the bilateral bottleneck.** Blend clipped row- and column-share evidence into the existing leg-support calculation, but continue to use the harmonic mean as a limiting factor. This tests whether routes backed by meaningful demand at both endpoints improve samples beyond the current combination of absolute leg evidence and affinity.

4. **Target the small-cut correction to vulnerable endpoints.** Keep the existing binary expansion and valid-topology offset removal, but scale each pair’s cut coefficient by a clipped endpoint-vulnerability factor, such as external demand relative to incident demand. Cut-aware designs have repeatedly produced strong repaired gaps; this mutation tests whether concentrating that signal on vulnerable nodes helps further.

5. **Tune route strength by network size and assess raw and repaired outcomes separately.** Sweep small changes to `route_size_factor` or its size scaling, especially for larger networks where feasibility declines and the largest raw gap occurs at \(n=16\). Keep direct rewards and cut terms fixed during the sweep, and compare sample quality as well as repaired gap so post-processing does not obscure weaker raw topologies.
