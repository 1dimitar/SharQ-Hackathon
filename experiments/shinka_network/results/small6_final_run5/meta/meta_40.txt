# INDIVIDUAL PROGRAM SUMMARIES

The following are summaries of individual programs evaluated since the last meta update:

**Program Name: Pressure-Discounted Two-Hop QUBO Surrogate**
- **Implementation**: Normalizes nonnegative, non-self demand and assigns negative linear weights using a demand/square-root blend. For each positive-demand pair, it selects a small set of two-hop routes by pressure-adjusted affinity, adds a low-pressure alternative, and returns a fixed penalty weight of 1.25.
- **Performance**: Combined score is 0.89 (raw 0.83, post-processed 0.96); mean best and median gaps are 11.66% and 32.42%, with 91% feasibility and 383 quadratic terms on average.
- **Feedback**: The surrogate builds quickly and produces consistently strong post-processed results, reducing the mean gap to 4.38%. Before repair, median gaps remain around 29–37%, and the largest best-sample gap is 16.8% for network14_s0, so its strongest contribution is as a head start for the fixed post-processor.

# GLOBAL INSIGHTS SCRATCHPAD

The following are global insights about optimization approaches and their effectiveness:

## Successful Algorithmic Patterns

- **The current best, Generation 8, remains the strongest overall quality–size balance.** It scores **0.90** (raw **0.8398**, post-processed **0.9607**), with **96% feasibility**, a **4.12% mean post-repair gap**, and **467.17 mean quadratic terms**. This matches or exceeds the combined scores of the new programs while using fewer terms than most of them.
- **Sparse, demand-supported two-hop rewards with transit-pressure ranking consistently produce strong repair-ready solutions.** Generation 8 ranks candidate routes by edge affinity discounted by node pressure, keeps two or four candidates based on demand, and adds one pressure-discounted alternative. Recent variants show the same broad pattern: Generation 36 scores **0.89** with a **3.80% post gap**, and Generation 37 scores **0.90** with **98% feasibility** and a **4.48% post gap**.
- **The current best’s direct-reward blend and reciprocal-link correction are effective together.** Its linear reward combines normalized demand and its square root (**0.75/0.25**), while its reciprocal-link penalty discourages backtracking but is reduced for substantial mutual traffic. The result is a **4.12% post gap** with a compact interaction model; newer programs also add diversity or congestion penalties, but do not show a consistent combined-score advantage.
- **Fixed post-processing is a major contributor to practical quality across generations.** Generation 8 improves from a **10.83% mean best-sample gap** to **4.12%** after repair. Generation 36 similarly reaches a **3.80%** post gap despite a **12.23%** raw best gap, and Generation 37 reaches **4.48%** from an **11.51%** raw best gap.

## Ineffective Approaches

- **Adding complexity or more route interactions has not reliably improved the score.** Generation 34 uses up to **3,894 quadratic terms** and scores **0.88**; Generation 38 averages **491–1,367 terms** and scores **0.89**; Generation 39 averages **534 terms** and also scores **0.89**. These results do not beat Generation 8’s **0.90** with about **467 terms**.
- **Congestion and route-selection refinements have not eliminated weak typical raw samples.** Generation 36 has a **31.40% median gap** despite a **12.23% mean best gap**; Generation 37 reports **28.92%** median versus **11.51%** best; Generation 38 reports **31.94%** median versus **13.14%** best. Generation 8 has the same broad limitation (**28.72%** median versus **10.83%** best), so good best candidates do not imply consistently strong samples.
- **High feasibility alone is not sufficient to improve overall results.** Generation 34 reaches **100% feasibility** but scores **0.88**. Generation 39 reaches **99% feasibility** but scores **0.89** and has a **5.19% post gap**, compared with Generation 8’s **96% feasibility**, **0.90** score, and **4.12%** post gap.
- **Adaptive or fixed constraint-weight changes have not demonstrated a clear advantage over the current best.** The recent programs use fixed weights of **1.25–1.30** or size-dependent penalties, but their combined scores remain **0.88–0.90** and their post gaps range from **3.80% to 5.19%**. The evaluation results do not isolate penalty weight as a cause of improvement.

## Implementation Insights

- **Generation 8 keeps the model sparse by selecting routes rather than expanding all possible two-hop paths.** For each positive-demand pair, it ranks intermediates using affinity divided by node pressure to the **0.75** power, keeps two or four candidates based on a demand quantile, and retains one alternative selected by pressure discount. This yields about **467 terms** on average, compared with Generation 37’s **329–897** and Generation 39’s **534** on average.
- **Its normalization and blended direct reward preserve moderate-demand links.** The implementation clips negative demand, clears the diagonal, normalizes by the maximum, and combines normalized demand with its square root at **0.75/0.25**. This limits domination by the largest demands while retaining nonzero rewards for smaller ones; the resulting raw score is **0.8398** and post score is **0.9607**.
- **The reciprocal-link penalty addresses a specific structural inefficiency without suppressing useful mutual traffic.** Generation 8 penalizes paired \(i\to j\) and \(j\to i\) links, scales the penalty with network depth, and discounts it when both directions have substantial demand. Its **4.12%** post gap is lower than Generation 39’s **5.19%**, despite the latter’s reciprocal-link and neighbor-overlap adjustments.
- **Recent implementations add useful safeguards and targeted interactions, but their extra terms have not established an overall advantage.** Generation 36 adds demand-profile redundancy penalties (fixed weight **1.25**); Generation 37 adds adaptive route, backtrack, and diversity penalties; Generation 38 adds reciprocal-link and neighbor-overlap adjustments. They remain competitive on repaired outcomes, but Generation 8 retains the best combination of score and compactness among the results summarized.

## Performance Analysis

- **Generation 8 remains the reference result:** combined score **0.90**, mean post gap **4.12%**, mean quadratic terms **467.17**, and feasibility **96%**. Generation 37 ties the **0.90** score but has a **4.48%** post gap and **98%** feasibility; Generation 36 has a slightly better **3.80%** post gap but scores **0.89**.
- **Recent programs cluster just below the current best in combined score.** Generation 34 scores **0.88**; Generations 36, 38, and 39 score **0.89**; Generation 37 scores **0.90**. Their reported post gaps range from **3.80%** (Generation 36) to **5.19%** (Generation 39), showing that the lowest post gap does not by itself determine the highest combined score.
- **Generation 8’s repair performance is strong across sizes but not uniform across instances.** Its post gaps range from **0.94%** on network12_s1 and **1.31%** on network10_s0 to **7.35%** on network13_s1; its largest raw best gap is **14.5%** on network15_s0. This reinforces that targeted route interactions and repair-ready candidate quality matter more than simply increasing constraints or model size.

# META RECOMMENDATIONS

The following are actionable recommendations for the next program generations:

1. **Add an ambiguity-based route candidate, not a blanket increase in route count.** Keep the existing two-or-four candidate rule, but include one additional candidate when its affinity-to-pressure rank is close to the last selected candidate. This tests whether extra flexibility helps uncertain pairs while preserving the sparse model that gives Generation 8 its strong score with about 467 quadratic terms.

2. **Require constituent-leg demand support for two-hop rewards.** Multiply each selected route’s existing endpoint-demand support by a bounded factor derived from normalized demand on both legs, so routes whose links carry little traffic receive less reward. Retain the current pressure discount and direct-reward blend; this targets route plausibility without disrupting the components associated with the best result.

3. **Apply a mild second-pass congestion adjustment to selected routes.** After route selection, accumulate their weighted use at each intermediate node and slightly discount rewards through the most-used nodes. Keep the adjustment small and local, building on the existing node-pressure ranking rather than adding broad route interactions, since larger and more elaborate models have not consistently improved combined score.

4. **Add at most one supported three-hop alternative for difficult pairs.** Consider a pair only when its best two-hop choices are weak or close in rank, require positive endpoint demand and support on all three legs, and discount by pressure at both intermediate nodes. This explores route flexibility while limiting term growth, unlike expanding paths indiscriminately.

5. **Target constraint strengthening at frequently coupled links instead of raising the global penalty.** Count how often each link appears in selected route-reward interactions, then add a modest penalty adjustment only to the most frequently coupled links while leaving the existing size-scaled baseline unchanged elsewhere. This tests a focused feasibility improvement without relying on uniformly stronger penalties, which have not shown a consistent score advantage.
