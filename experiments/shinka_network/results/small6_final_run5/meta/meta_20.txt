# GLOBAL INSIGHTS SCRATCHPAD

The following are global insights about optimization approaches and their effectiveness:

## Successful Algorithmic Patterns
- **The current best, Generation 8’s Demand-Aware Sparse QUBO Surrogate, remains a strong quality–size balance.** It scores **0.90** (sample-quality **0.8398**, head-start **0.9607**) with a **4.12% mean post-processed gap**, **96% feasibility**, and about **467 quadratic terms**. Its normalized direct-demand rewards, sparse pressure-discounted two-hop rewards, and reciprocal-link penalty align with the consistently strong sparse-routing pattern seen in earlier generations.
- **Demand-supported, pressure-aware route rewards recur among the strongest new results.** Generation 17’s Pressure-Weighted Sparse Route QUBO also scores **0.90**, with **98% feasibility**, **11.89% mean best gap**, and **4.04% post gap** using sparse selected two-hop routes and overlap corrections. Generation 16’s Pressure-Discounted QUBO Surrogate scores **0.89** with a **4.53% post gap**, while Generation 19’s Demand-Aware Surrogate scores **0.88** with a **4.71% post gap**. Together with Generation 8, these results support rewarding routes that have demand evidence while discounting congested transit nodes.
- **Repair-ready samples matter alongside raw sample quality.** Generation 8’s mean best gap of **10.83%** becomes **4.12%** after repair; Generation 17 similarly moves from **11.89%** to **4.04%**. This complements prior evidence from Generation 11, whose gap fell from **12.10%** to **3.98%**. The strongest programs provide useful topologies for the fixed post-processor even though their typical raw samples are substantially weaker.

## Ineffective Approaches
- **More elaborate or denser route models have not reliably surpassed the sparse best.** Generation 18’s Load-Aware Surrogate scores **0.86**, with **17.67% mean best gap**, **39.78% median gap**, and **6.31% post gap**, despite generating **720–3,360** terms. Generation 19 produces **838–3,672** terms and scores **0.88**, below Generation 8’s **0.90** at about 467 terms. This is consistent with earlier findings that larger quadratic models do not automatically improve score.
- **Adding three-hop modeling did not eliminate weak raw samples.** Generation 16 includes selected three-hop terms but has a **32.45% raw median gap** and a worst best-sample gap of **17.4%** on network16_s1; its **4.53% post gap** is good but not better than Generation 8’s **4.12%**. The additional route depth therefore did not produce a clear aggregate advantage in these results.
- **Strong repair results can mask substantial raw-sample variability.** Generation 8 has a **28.72% median gap**, while the new Generation 19 reaches **34.48%** and Generation 16 reaches **32.45%**. Generation 18 combines a **39.78%** median gap with only **97% feasibility**. These results show that high post-processed quality does not imply consistently strong or valid sampled topologies.
- **Feasibility and best-sample quality weaken on particular instances or sizes.** The current best’s feasibility ranges from **91%** on network15_s2 to **98%** on several cases, and its worst raw gap is **14.5%** on network15_s0. Generation 19’s worst raw gap is **20.8%** on network15_s0; Generation 18 reaches **22.3%**. Demand-aware terms alone do not remove difficult-instance failures.

## Implementation Insights
- **Generation 8’s normalization and blended direct reward are a stable foundation.** It clips negative demand, clears the diagonal, scales by maximum demand, and combines normalized demand with its square root using weights **0.75/0.25**. This gives direct rewards comparable scales while retaining some value for moderate-demand links; the model achieves **0.90** with about **467 terms**.
- **Its route selection uses both demand evidence and transit pressure.** For each positive-demand pair, it ranks candidate intermediates using edge affinity discounted by node pressure to the power **0.75**, keeps two or four candidates depending on demand, and adds one alternative favored by a full-path pressure discount. This targeted route construction helps keep the model sparse while supporting a **4.12%** mean repaired gap.
- **The reciprocal-link term targets an identifiable structural inefficiency.** Generation 8 penalizes reciprocal links because an \(i \to j \to i\) path carries no source-to-destination traffic, while reducing the penalty when demand is substantial in both directions. The penalty is scaled modestly with network depth; this accompanies **96% feasibility** and a **0.9607** head-start component.
- **The newer implementations show useful alternatives, but not a consistent gain over the best.** Generation 17 selects two-hop routes based on demand support and node pressure and adds selective overlap corrections, reaching **4.04%** post gap with an average of **626 terms**. Generation 16 selectively adds three-hop terms for difficult or high-demand pairs, but its **4.53%** post gap and weak raw median do not improve on Generation 8’s overall balance.

## Performance Analysis
- **Generation 8 remains at the top combined score among the programs summarized here.** Its **0.90** score is matched by Generation 17 (**0.90**); Generation 17 has a slightly lower post gap (**4.04%** versus **4.12%**) and higher feasibility (**98%** versus **96%**), while Generation 8 has the slightly stronger reported sample-quality/head-start split and fewer terms (about **467** versus **626**). The results are close rather than a clear win for either on every metric.
- **New programs mostly achieve good repaired gaps without exceeding the current best score.** Generation 16 scores **0.89** with a **4.53%** post gap; Generation 19 scores **0.88** with **4.71%**; Generation 18 scores **0.86** with **6.31%**. Generation 15 scores **0.87**, with **5.67%** post gap. Thus repair quality remains strong across designs, but the current best’s aggregate balance remains difficult to beat.
- **The current best’s repaired performance varies meaningfully by instance.** Its post gaps range from **0.94%** on network12_s1 to **7.35%** on network13_s1; raw best gaps peak at **14.5%** on network15_s0. Despite these outliers, its mean post gap is **4.12%** and head-start score is **0.9607**.
- **Quadratic-term count alone is not predictive of quality.** Generation 8 reaches **0.90** with roughly **467 terms**, and Generation 17 reaches **0.90** with roughly **626**; denser Generation 18 and Generation 19 models score **0.86** and **0.88**, respectively. This reinforces the earlier comparisons, where sparse Generation 14 achieved **0.90** with **184–467 terms**, while denser alternatives did not consistently improve the score.

# META RECOMMENDATIONS

The following are actionable recommendations for the next program generations:

1. **Adapt the number of retained two-hop routes to how clearly the candidates rank.** Keep the current two-or-four route allowance as a baseline, but retain additional candidates only when the top affinity-to-pressure scores are close; when one route clearly dominates, keep fewer. This tests whether terms concentrated on ambiguous choices improve over the current sparse model without increasing density uniformly.

2. **Estimate transit pressure from the selected routes and use it to refine their rewards.** After selecting candidate routes, accumulate each intermediate node’s weighted transit demand, then modestly discount rewards for routes through the most heavily loaded nodes. This builds on the successful pressure-aware routing pattern while making its congestion estimate specific to the chosen route set.

3. **Make each two-hop reward depend explicitly on the strength of both legs.** Scale the existing endpoint-demand support by a bounded function of normalized demand on \(i\to j\) and \(j\to k\), so routes with a weak leg receive less reward; retain the existing pressure discount and test conservative weight changes. Demand- and pressure-supported sparse routes recur among the strongest results, while this refinement can avoid rewarding indirect paths on endpoint demand alone.

4. **Add a tightly capped three-hop alternative only for difficult endpoint pairs.** Preserve two-hop routes as the foundation, and consider one three-hop route only when the endpoint has high demand and its leading two-hop candidates are weak or closely tied; rank it using leg support and intermediate-node pressure. Generation 16’s broader three-hop modeling did not beat the current best, so keep this experiment targeted and sparse rather than adding depth globally.

5. **Strengthen constraint penalties selectively on links with many reward interactions.** Count how often each link appears in selected route-reward terms and apply a modest penalty increase only to the most coupled links, leaving other links near the current size-scaled penalty. This targets feasibility weaknesses such as network15_s2’s 91% rate while avoiding a uniform increase that could suppress useful demand rewards.
