# GLOBAL INSIGHTS SCRATCHPAD

The following are global insights about optimization approaches and their effectiveness:

## Successful Algorithmic Patterns
- **A quality-and-diversity archive combined with broad, screened local search remains the strongest pattern.** The current best, **Diverse Archive Topology Local Search (Generation 16)**, achieves the best reported result: **0.99 score and 1.37% mean gap** in **15.08 seconds**. Its archive retains strong networks alongside structurally different alternatives, while its degree-preserving switches and three-edge rotations explore multiple neighborhoods. This outperforms the newer archive-based programs: **Archive-Guided Bottleneck Network Search (Generation 28)** scored **0.98 with a 2.06% gap**, and **Diversity-Preserving Topology Genetic Search (Generation 22)** scored **0.98 with a 2.07% gap**.
- **Surrogate ranking is most effective when used to allocate LP calls, not to eliminate exploration.** Generation 16 sorts valid proposals by `tools.energy`, evaluates batches of at most nine, and sometimes includes a randomized tail candidate. This retains exploration while screening expensive LP evaluations. Generation 28 also screens batches and reaches a similar score, but has a wider reported gap range (**0.40%–3.64%**) and takes about twice as long (**29.98 seconds**).
- **LP price/load guidance works well as one move-generation signal within a broader search.** Generation 16 uses positive LP prices or high loads to target switches and rotations, then retries unrestricted moves if guided proposals run dry. The related **Dual-Guided Network Neighborhood Search (Generation 27)** also performed strongly (**0.98 score, 2.06% gap**) with price-guided switches, targeted rotations, and random kicks. This contrasts with Generation 29, where dual-guided moves plus stagnation-triggered rotations reached only **0.95 and a 5.75% gap**.
- **Repaired QAOA starts can improve outcomes, but their value depends on the search.** Generation 16’s QAOA starts averaged **0.9865 best-known/best**, versus **0.9722** for random starts. Generation 25 also showed a consistent QAOA advantage across its three seeds (**0.9727 vs. 0.9607**). These gains support using repaired QAOA candidates as a useful seed pool, but do not by themselves account for final search quality.

## Ineffective Approaches
- **More search machinery or move types do not reliably improve results.** Generation 29’s guided switches, peak-load targeting, and randomized rotations scored **0.95 with a 5.75% gap**, well below Generation 16’s **0.99 and 1.37%**. Earlier results show the same warning: Generation 19’s dual-ranked switches, annealing, and kicks reached a **4.61% gap**, and Generation 21’s two-arc swaps and cyclic reassignment reached **6.45%**.
- **QAOA initialization advantages do not guarantee strong final solutions.** Generation 25’s QAOA starts beat random starts on all three seeds, yet its mean gap was **2.80%**, versus Generation 16’s **1.37%**. Generation 22 likewise had mixed seed outcomes and only a marginal QAOA-versus-random advantage (**0.9797 vs. 0.9790**) despite a **2.07% gap**. Initialization quality is therefore not a reliable proxy for search quality.
- **Dual guidance without sufficiently effective exploration and archive behavior can underperform.** Generation 29’s QAOA and random starts were nearly indistinguishable (**0.9459 vs. 0.9465 best-known/best**), and its final gap was **5.75%**. More broadly, previous dual-guided variants reached gaps of **4.61%–6.45%** despite using the full LP budget. Guidance alone did not match the stronger archive-plus-screening results.

## Implementation Insights
- **Generation 16’s archive explicitly balances elite quality and topology diversity.** It keeps up to 18 scored networks, protects strong candidates, and retains alternatives based on edge-set symmetric difference. Parent selection draws from high-ranked archive members and, during stagnation, sometimes from more distant members. This supports both exploitation and escape from a narrow incumbent basin, and is consistent with its leading **1.37% mean gap**.
- **The current best preserves exploration at several stages.** It selects diverse repaired seeds before filling remaining seed slots, uses both destination swaps and three-edge rotations, and falls back from price/load-targeted moves to unrestricted moves when needed. These implementation choices avoid depending on one initialization, move operator, or guidance signal.
- **LP evaluation is carefully separated from cheap proposal screening.** Generation 16 canonicalizes and caches networks, rejects already-scored or cached proposals, orders fresh moves by surrogate energy, and evaluates small batches under the LP budget. The energy score is an ordering signal rather than a hard cutoff, with occasional randomized alternatives. This is consistent with its lower runtime (**15.08 seconds**) than the similarly strong Generation 27 (**49.42 seconds**) and Generation 28 (**29.98 seconds**), all reported at about 2,000 LP calls.
- **The current best improves substantially over a weaker use of similar guidance.** Generation 29 also uses price/load or peak-load information to generate moves and maintains a diverse beam, but its **5.75% gap** indicates that guidance and diversity alone are not sufficient. Generation 16’s combination of a quality-protecting archive, multiple move types, unrestricted fallback, and screened LP batches distinguishes the stronger implementation pattern.

## Performance Analysis
- **Generation 16 remains the clear leader:** **0.99 combined score, 1.37% mean gap, 0.97 control score, and 15.08 seconds** at **2,000 LP calls**. The strongest new results are Generation 27 (**0.98, 2.06%, 49.42 seconds**), Generation 28 (**0.98, 2.06%, 29.98 seconds**), and Generation 22 (**0.98, 2.07%, 123.34 seconds**). Their similar gaps are roughly 0.7 percentage points worse and their runtimes are higher.
- **Results support archive quality and search breadth over LP-call count alone.** Generation 16, Generations 22, 27, and 28, and weaker dual-guided programs all used about **2,000 LP calls**, yet their gaps ranged from **1.37%** to **5.75%**. Full budget use did not prevent weaker searches from finishing farther from the best-known solutions.
- **Generation 16’s seed-level results are strong but not uniform.** Its QAOA-start gaps were **0.94%, 1.53%, and 1.64%**, compared with random-start gaps of **3.68%, 3.40%, and 1.53%**. QAOA substantially helped on two seeds but narrowly lost on seed 2, matching the broader pattern of seed sensitivity in the other programs.
- **Strong QAOA-versus-control differences do not predict the best absolute result.** Generation 25’s initialization advantage (**0.9727 vs. 0.9607**) accompanied a **2.80% gap**, while Generation 28’s larger advantage (**0.9800 vs. 0.9622**) accompanied a **2.06% gap**; neither surpassed Generation 16. Final quality tracks the search and evaluation strategy more closely than the initialization comparison alone.

# META RECOMMENDATIONS

The following are actionable recommendations for the next program generations:

1. **Make each LP-evaluation batch topology-diverse as well as energy-ranked.** After sorting fresh moves by `tools.energy`, greedily add proposals whose edge-set symmetric difference from already selected batch members exceeds a threshold; fill any remaining slots with the best-ranked moves. This extends the successful archive-diversity strategy to expensive LP calls and can reduce redundant evaluations without making energy a hard filter.

2. **Reserve batch slots for moves with strong predicted LP-price benefit.** For each proposed switch or rotation, compute the sum of prices on removed arcs minus the sum on added arcs, then use that signal alongside `tools.energy` to rank proposals or reserve a few slots for high-benefit moves. LP-price guidance already helps generate relevant neighborhoods, while Generation 16’s screened batches provide a natural place to test whether directional price information improves selection.

3. **Adapt parent selection using measured search productivity, while preserving exploration.** Track each archive member’s evaluations, improvements, and best gain, and sample productive parents more often; keep a fixed share of searches for less-tested or topologically distant members. This could allocate the fixed LP budget more effectively while retaining the diverse archive behavior associated with the leading result.

4. **Escalate three-edge rotations selectively after parent-level stagnation.** Track unsuccessful batches for each parent and, after several failures, increase the fraction of rotations touching that parent’s highest-price arcs; return to the normal switch/rotation mix when the parent yields an improvement. This tests a more targeted use of a move type already present in the best program without adding expensive rotations uniformly.

5. **Use bounded basin-change kicks from strong, diverse archive members during global stagnation.** After a sustained run without improving the incumbent, select a high-quality but non-elite archive member and apply a small number of degree-preserving switches before resuming ordinary search; increase kick size only if stagnation continues. This builds on the existing adaptive kick mechanism and archive diversity to escape a stalled basin without abandoning strong alternatives.
