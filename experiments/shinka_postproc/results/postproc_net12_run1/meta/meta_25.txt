# GLOBAL INSIGHTS SCRATCHPAD

The following are global insights about optimization approaches and their effectiveness:

## Successful Algorithmic Patterns
- **Diverse archives paired with screened LP evaluation remain the strongest pattern.** The current best, **Diverse Archive Topology Local Search (Generation 16)**, scored **0.99** with a **1.37% mean gap** in **15.08 seconds**. It combines a quality-and-diversity archive, degree-preserving switches and rotations, LP-price/load guidance, and energy-ranked batches with randomized alternatives. It leads the newer archive variants: Generation 24 scored **0.98** at **2.19% gap**, and Generation 26 scored **0.98** at **2.46% gap**.
- **Repaired QAOA seeds help on average, but their benefit depends on the search and varies by seed.** Generation 16’s QAOA starts averaged **0.9865 best-known/best**, versus **0.9722** for random starts; QAOA won on seeds 0 and 1 but narrowly lost on seed 2. Generation 26 showed a larger initialization advantage (**0.976 vs. 0.9303**) yet achieved a worse **2.46% gap**, illustrating that good starts do not replace effective search.
- **Price/load guidance works best as a targeting signal within a broader search.** The current best uses positive LP prices, falls back to high loads, and retries without hot-link restrictions when targeted moves run dry. Generation 18’s bottleneck-guided search also reached **0.98 score and 1.97% gap**. These results support guidance combined with broader exploration rather than guidance alone.

## Ineffective Approaches
- **Adding neighborhood types and search machinery does not guarantee better results.** Generation 19’s two-switch search with dual ranking, annealing, kicks, and an archive reached **0.96 score and 4.61% mean gap**; Generation 21’s two-arc swaps and cyclic reassignment reached **0.94 and 6.45%**. Both trail Generation 16, despite using the full 2,000-call budget.
- **Dual guidance and QAOA initialization alone are insufficient.** Generation 23 used LP-guided swaps and rotations with QAOA starts, but reached **0.94 score and 6.00% gap**. Generations 19 and 21 also reported QAOA advantages over random starts while missing the best-known solutions. Aggregate initialization gains therefore do not establish strong final search quality.
- **A strong QAOA-versus-random gap does not reliably predict the best absolute outcome.** Generation 26 had a substantial start advantage but a **2.46% mean gap**, compared with Generation 16’s **1.37%**. Likewise, seed reversals occur even for Generation 16: random starts slightly beat QAOA on seed 2 (**1.53% vs. 1.64% gap**).

## Implementation Insights
- **Generation 16’s archive balances elite quality with topology diversity.** Its implementation keeps up to 18 scored networks, protects strong candidates, and retains alternatives based on edge-set difference. Parent selection can draw from diverse archive members rather than repeatedly following only the incumbent. This is consistent with its leading **1.37% gap**, compared with Generation 24’s **2.19%** using a related quality-and-diversity archive.
- **Cheap surrogate ordering is used to allocate expensive LP calls without hard-filtering exploration.** Generation 16 sorts fresh valid proposals by `tools.energy`, evaluates batches capped at nine, and sometimes adds a randomized tail candidate. By contrast, its LP-guided move generation targets priced or loaded links but falls back to unrestricted moves if targeted proposals produce no fresh candidates. This combines focus with protection against narrow or misleading rankings.
- **The current best combines complementary move operators rather than relying on a single proposal mechanism.** It generates degree-preserving destination swaps and three-edge rotations, validates the resulting topologies, and searches from both repaired candidate seeds and archive parents. This contrasts with weaker results from searches centered on two-switches or swaps alone (Generations 19 and 21), though the evaluations do not isolate the effect of each component.
- **The full LP budget was common, so budget use alone does not explain the quality differences.** Generation 16 used 2,000 calls in **15.08 seconds**; Generations 19, 21, 23, 24, and 26 also used about 2,000 calls but took roughly **21.98–33 seconds** and achieved worse gaps. The current implementation therefore delivered better reported quality and runtime under a comparable call allowance.

## Performance Analysis
- **Generation 16 remains the clear best in the reported comparison:** **0.99 score, 1.37% mean gap, 0.97 control score, and 15.08 seconds**. The newer closest results are Generation 24 (**0.98, 2.19%, 29.91 seconds**) and Generation 26 (**0.98, 2.46%, 32.19 seconds**); both are about a percentage point worse in gap and substantially slower.
- **The newer dual-guided variants are materially weaker on final gaps.** Generation 19 reached **4.61%**, Generation 21 **6.45%**, and Generation 23 **6.00%**, versus Generation 16’s **1.37%**. Their QAOA starts often beat their own random controls on average, but this did not close the absolute-quality gap.
- **Generation 16’s per-seed outcomes are strong and mostly consistent:** QAOA-start gaps were **0.94%, 1.53%, and 1.64%**, compared with random-start gaps of **3.68%, 3.40%, and 1.53%**. This shows substantial QAOA gains on two seeds, alongside the same seed-level variability seen in other evaluations.
- **Across the evaluated programs, quality tracks the combination of archive diversity, selective evaluation, and broad enough exploration more closely than move count or initialization advantage alone.** Generation 16 leads on both gap and runtime; archive-based Generations 24 and 26 remain strong but do not match it, while more elaborate dual-guided searches can consume the full budget yet finish substantially farther from the best-known solutions.

# META RECOMMENDATIONS

The following are actionable recommendations for the next program generations:

1. **Make each LP batch topology-diverse as well as energy-ranked.** After sorting proposals by `tools.energy`, greedily retain candidates with sufficient edge-set difference from those already in the batch, then fill unused slots with the best remaining proposals. This extends the archive’s successful diversity strategy to expensive evaluations and reduces redundant calls while preserving the energy signal.

2. **Combine surrogate energy with explicit LP-price benefit when ordering proposals.** For each switch or rotation, calculate the sum of prices on removed links minus the sum on added links; reserve batch slots for candidates favored by energy, price benefit, or both. Price guidance already helps target bottlenecks, but using directional benefit for ranking may distinguish moves that relieve them from moves that merely touch priced links.

3. **Allocate parent searches using measured productivity while protecting exploration.** Track each archive member’s LP evaluations and improvements, sample productive parents more often, and reserve a fixed share of iterations for less-tested or topologically distant members. The current diverse archive is a major strength; productivity-based allocation could use the fixed budget more efficiently without collapsing the search onto the incumbent.

4. **Trigger stronger rotations selectively after parent-level stagnation.** Track unsuccessful batches per parent and, after several failures, increase three-edge rotations centered on that parent’s highest-price links; return to the ordinary mix after improvement. This builds on the existing rotation operator and price guidance while avoiding the cost of generating extra rotations uniformly.

5. **Add bounded basin-change kicks from diverse archive members during prolonged global stagnation.** After a sustained period without improving the incumbent, apply a small number of degree-preserving switches to a strong but non-elite archive member, increasing the perturbation only if progress remains stalled. This extends the current adaptive kick mechanism while retaining the archive alternatives that distinguish the best program from weaker, narrower searches.
