- **The current best, Generation 20 (“Diverse Energy- and Routing-Guided Search”), remains the strongest result:** combined score **0.99**, mean gap **1.04%**, and best gap **−0.61%**, using all 2,000 LP calls. Its mix of QUBO energy, demand-routing features, online ridge prediction, novelty, and uncertainty selection outperforms the newer, narrower screening variants.
- **Combining complementary rankings and reserving evaluation capacity for exploration are consistently associated with the strongest results.** Generation 20 explicitly allocates batch slots among learned predictions, energy, routing proxy, novelty, and uncertainty. Generation 31 (“Demand-Aware Surrogate-Guided Topology Search”) also reached **0.99**, with a **1.49%** mean gap, using demand features and a regularized predictor while reserving LP evaluations for promising, diverse, and uncertain candidates. Generation 33 reached **0.99 / 1.33%** with similar multi-signal guidance.
- **Degree-preserving moves paired with an archive maintain quality across topology variations.** Generation 20 uses two-switches, three-arc rotations, and larger perturbations with archive-based parent selection. Its mean gaps stay below 1.5% at every size: **1.34% (n=13), 0.34% (n=14), 1.48% (n=15), and 0.99% (n=16)**. The current best’s especially strong n=14 result reinforces the value of broad, diverse search rather than one move or ranking signal.

## Ineffective Approaches
- **Energy- and novelty-focused screening alone remains weaker than the current best’s multi-signal selection.** Generation 30’s surrogate-ranked archive and screened two-switch/rotation/kick search scored **0.97** with a **2.60%** mean gap. Generation 32 added tie-robust routing features but still scored **0.98 / 2.15%**. Both used all 2,000 LP calls, so their weaker results reflect search and evaluation selection quality, not fewer calls.
- **Adding routing and uncertainty guidance does not by itself eliminate variability or exceed Generation 20.** Generation 31 scored **0.99 / 1.49%** and Generation 33 **0.99 / 1.33%**, both above Generation 20’s mean gap, despite finding optimum-level results in individual runs. Their feedback reports substantial seed and pool variation; Generation 33 ranged from a **−0.12%** best gap to weaker size-level performance, including **1.80% at n=13**.
- **Validation failure makes a plausible search design unevaluable.** Generation 34’s “Diverse Archive QAOA Post-Processor” received **0.0** because the submitted code contained the forbidden `signal` construct. Its LP dual/load, congestion, and diversity-guided moves therefore demonstrated no valid performance.

## Implementation Insights
- **Generation 20’s routing features provide distinct inputs to screening and prediction.** Its nine features combine energy with demand-weighted distance, maximum and upper-tail link loads, load RMS, maximum source/target work, direct traffic, and unreachable pairs. Batched APSP and equal splitting over shortest paths supply a cheap routing proxy alongside the Hamiltonian energy.
- **The online ridge predictor is effective as one component of a protected portfolio, not as the sole selector.** Generation 20 calibrates predictions within each run and uses uncertainty, while explicitly reserving batch choices for energy, routing proxy, and novelty. Generation 31 similarly combines a predictor with demand features and reserved evaluations, but its **1.49%** mean gap shows that this general design does not guarantee Generation 20’s better aggregate result.
- **Diversity is enforced at multiple stages in the current best.** Repaired initial networks are interleaved between energy and demand-proxy rankings and filtered for near-duplicates; archive and LP-batch selection also enforce topology separation. This complements Generation 20’s score-focused elite archive and helps preserve alternative structures during search.
- **LP feedback guides move generation as well as evaluation.** Generation 20 uses positive LP prices, falling back to high loads, to identify hot arcs for proposals. Newer programs also used LP signals—Generation 32 screened on robust routing features and Generation 34 proposed dual/load signals—but Generation 32’s **2.15%** gap indicates that such signals are most effective when combined with broader prediction, diversity, and batch-allocation mechanisms.

## Performance Analysis
- **Generation 20 remains the best evaluated program by mean gap:** **1.04%**, versus Generation 33 at **1.33%**, Generation 31 at **1.49%**, Generation 32 at **2.15%**, and Generation 30 at **2.60%**. The score is **0.99** for Generations 20, 31, and 33, but the mean gaps distinguish their quality.
- **Generation 20’s size-level results are both strong and comparatively even.** Its gaps range from **0.34% at n=14** to **1.48% at n=15**. Generation 33 is strong at n=14 (**0.69%**) but weaker at n=13 (**1.80%**); Generation 30 ranges from **2.03% at n=16** to **3.32% at n=15**. Generation 31’s reported size means range from **1.07% at n=14** to **1.84% at n=15**.
- **Best-run quality and aggregate reliability differ.** Generation 33 reached a best gap of **−0.12%**, and Generation 31 matched a known optimum, while Generation 20’s best gap was **−0.61%**. But Generation 20 has the lowest mean gap among these strong programs and remains below 1.5% at all four sizes. Its per-Hamiltonian best results were **+0.40% for r2g181** and **+0.63% for r3g128**.
- **All successful programs used the full 2,000-call LP budget, so call count does not explain their performance differences.** Runtime also does not track quality monotonically: Generation 20 averaged **48.61 seconds**, Generation 31 **41.93 seconds**, Generation 33 **45.95 seconds**, Generation 32 **53.24 seconds**, and Generation 30 **34.56 seconds**. The faster Generation 30 and slower Generation 32 both had substantially larger mean gaps than Generation 20.

# META RECOMMENDATIONS

The following are actionable recommendations for the next program generations:

1. **Adapt batch quotas using observed improvement rates.** Track how often candidates selected by the learned, energy, routing-proxy, novelty, and uncertainty rankings improve the incumbent, then adjust each signal’s next-round allocation modestly while preserving at least one slot for every signal. This extends Generation 20’s successful portfolio selection without allowing short-term results to eliminate exploration.

2. **Make bottleneck-guided moves explicitly remove hot arcs.** For each arc with a positive LP price—or, when prices are unavailable, a high load—propose compatible two-switches and three-arc rotations that remove that arc, alongside the existing untargeted moves. This turns Generation 20’s useful LP feedback for parent and move prioritization into more direct topology changes while retaining broad search.

3. **Add a tie-robust routing signal without replacing the existing proxy.** For shortlisted candidates, recompute routing loads under a few deterministic shortest-path tie-breakings or splits, and include a high-percentile or worst-case load summary in the routing ranking. Use it as one additional selection signal rather than a filter, preserving the complementary energy, predictor, novelty, and uncertainty choices that outperform narrower screening.

4. **Maintain explicit archive coverage across quality signals.** Reserve archive slots for LP elites, low-energy candidates, routing-proxy leaders, and structurally novel topologies, and sample parents across these groups, particularly after stalls. This builds on the current distance-filtered archive while reducing the chance that LP-ranked elites or one topology region gradually dominate future proposals.

5. **Adapt perturbation scale to progress and size.** After repeated non-improving rounds, increase multi-switch kicks or steps toward distant archive members; after an improvement, favor local switches and rotations, especially around hot arcs. Test a modestly higher exploration allowance for n=13 and n=15, whose mean gaps are the weakest, while keeping the current mix of local moves and broad perturbations for other sizes.
