# INDIVIDUAL PROGRAM SUMMARIES

The following are summaries of individual programs evaluated since the last meta update:

**Program Name: Demand-Weighted Linear Topology Surrogate**
- **Implementation**: Assigns each directed link a linear weight equal to negative demand normalized by the matrix maximum, with no quadratic terms and a degree-penalty weight of 1.0. The evaluator receives the terms and runtime through a fixed entry point.
- **Performance**: Combined score was 0.80; mean best gap was 12.76%, median gap 40.37%, feasibility 100%, and the model used zero quadratic terms.
- **Feedback**: All cases produced 64 distinct topologies; three had 100% valid samples before repair, while network10_s3 had 98%. Best gaps ranged from 10.7% to 15.9%, with network10_s3 worst, indicating the simple demand-only surrogate yields valid but suboptimal topologies.

**Program Name: Demand-Weighted Two-Hop Surrogate QUBO**
- **Implementation**: Assigns negative linear weights to directed links in proportion to demand, then adds negative quadratic rewards for two-hop paths, discounted by transit-node pressure and direct edge demand. Returns a degree-penalty weight of 1.0; construction is efficient for the tested 10-node networks.
- **Performance**: Combined score was 0.81, with a 12.30% mean best gap, 36.41% mean median gap, 0.99 feasible rate, and 480 quadratic terms.
- **Feedback**: The method produced topologies close to the reference best, but median gaps were substantially larger; the largest best gap was 15.6% on network10_s1. It generated 64 distinct topologies per case and built in under 0.01 seconds.

# GLOBAL INSIGHTS SCRATCHPAD

The following are global insights about optimization approaches and their effectiveness:

## Successful Algorithmic Patterns
- **Generation 57, *Pressure-Discounted Two-Hop QUBO Surrogate*, remains the strongest overall result in the supplied comparisons.** It scores **0.83**, with an **11.66%** mean best gap and **30.61%** mean median gap. Its combination of normalized direct-demand rewards and two-hop rewards discounted for transit-node pressure and demand on component edges is the clearest high-performing pattern.
- **The current best’s median-gap advantage is especially notable.** Its **30.61%** median gap beats Generation 53’s **31.47%**, Generation 40’s **33.37%**, and Generation 14’s **35.22%**. Its best gap is competitive, though not the lowest historical result: Generation 44 reaches **10.30%**, Generation 52 **10.40%**, and Generation 14 **10.53%**.
- **Pressure-discounted paths also perform well in the current evaluation set.** Generation 56, *Pressure-Discounted Two-Hop QUBO Surrogate*, scores **0.82**, with a **9.66%** mean best gap, **35.73%** median gap, and **98%** feasibility. It has the lowest best gap among the newly supplied programs, while Generation 57 has the better typical-sample gap and score.
- **Generation 57’s case results show that the pattern can produce strong best solutions, but performance varies by instance.** Its best gap is **6.5%** on `network10_s2`; the other cases range from **13.3% to 13.6%**. Median gaps range from **28.5% to 35.1%**, so its aggregate advantage does not mean uniformly strong results.

## Ineffective Approaches
- **Adding endpoint-traffic features to direct rewards did not improve results in these evaluations.** Generation 54 combines direct traffic, its square root, and endpoint throughput, but scores **0.83** with an **10.91%** best gap and **33.72%** median gap. Its score matches Generation 57’s, but its median gap is 3.11 points higher; it also uses **1,200** quadratic terms rather than 480.
- **The other demand-guided variants show substantially weaker typical-sample quality than Generation 57.** Generation 58 scores **0.80** with a **13.24%** best gap and **38.70%** median gap; Generation 60 scores **0.81** with **12.75%** and **37.18%** gaps. Both report 100% feasibility, but neither matches Generation 57’s gap results.
- **The simpler pressure-weighted path formulation was weaker than the current best.** Generation 55 scores **0.80**, with **14.33%** best and **39.13%** median gaps. Its direct-demand linear rewards and pressure-adjusted two-hop support produced 480 terms, but its gaps are worse than Generation 57’s by 2.67 and 8.52 percentage points, respectively.
- **High feasibility alone did not correspond to the best gaps.** Generations 54, 58, and 60 report **100%** feasibility, and Generation 56 reports **98%**; Generation 57 reports **90%**. However, Generation 57 has the best median gap among these results. Its feasibility is better than Generation 53’s **0.82**, but below the fully feasible variants.

## Implementation Insights
- **Generation 57 uses a normalized direct-demand reward:** it clips demand at zero, removes diagonal traffic, scales by the maximum off-diagonal demand, and assigns each directed edge `-traffic[i,j]/scale`. This keeps the direct-service signal simple; by contrast, Generation 54 adds square-root demand and endpoint throughput, and has a worse median gap despite the same score.
- **Its two-hop reward applies two explicit discounts.** The implementation multiplies normalized OD demand by `path_strength = 0.56`, then divides by `node_pressure[j] * (1 + 0.75 * edge_pressure)`. This rewards demand-supported paths while reducing rewards for busy intermediates and component links with substantial direct demand. Generation 56 also uses pressure-discounted paths and has a better best gap, but a higher median gap (**35.73%**).
- **Generation 57’s pressure normalization and term construction are concrete and lightweight.** Incident demand is normalized by its mean, with a node-pressure floor of **0.75**. Canonical edge-pair keys produce **480 quadratic terms**, versus **1,200** in the endpoint-throughput variants. Construction takes under **0.01 seconds**.
- **Degree constraints are delegated to the evaluator.** Generation 57 returns a fixed penalty weight of **1.0** rather than embedding degree penalties in its surrogate. Despite its strong gap metrics, its pre-repair feasibility is only **88–95%** across the four cases, so feasibility and objective quality remain separate characteristics of this implementation.

## Performance Analysis
- **Generation 57 remains the leading balanced result in the supplied comparisons.** It scores **0.83**, tying Generation 53’s score, and has a lower median gap (**30.61%** versus **31.47%**). Its mean feasibility (**0.90**) is also above Generation 53’s **0.82**, though below the 98–100% reported by several newer variants.
- **Generation 56 has the strongest best-gap result among the new programs, but not the strongest overall profile.** Its **9.66%** mean best gap is better than Generation 57’s **11.66%**, but its score is **0.82** and median gap **35.73%**, compared with **0.83** and **30.61%** for Generation 57.
- **The new demand-weighted and pressure-weighted alternatives generally trail Generation 57 on median gap.** Generation 54’s median gap is **33.72%**; Generations 55, 58, and 60 report **39.13%**, **38.70%**, and **37.18%**, respectively. These results reinforce that strong best samples or perfect feasibility do not necessarily translate into strong median performance.
- **Runtime, topology diversity, and term count do not explain the quality differences.** The reported programs all build in under **0.01 seconds** and produce 64 distinct topologies per case. Generation 54’s 1,200 terms do not yield a better median gap than Generation 57’s 480-term model, while Generation 55’s same 480-term count also does not ensure comparable gaps; reward design is the more relevant distinction in these results.

# META RECOMMENDATIONS

The following are actionable recommendations for the next program generations:

1. **Sweep the two-hop reward strength around `path_strength = 0.56`**, testing values such as `0.46`, `0.50`, `0.62`, and `0.68` while holding the direct rewards and pressure formula fixed. This isolates the indirect-reward budget in the strongest observed design and may improve the weaker cases without losing its median-gap advantage.

2. **Test alternative transit-node pressure scaling** while keeping the two-hop reward structure unchanged: compare mean incident demand with maximum incident demand for normalization, and try pressure floors of `0.65` and `0.85` against the current `0.75`. Pressure-discounted paths perform well, but case-to-case variation—particularly the weaker `network10_s1` result—suggests the pressure calibration may benefit from tuning.

3. **Vary the component-edge pressure discount** in the two-hop denominator, for example comparing `1 + 0.5 * edge_pressure`, the current `1 + 0.75 * edge_pressure`, and `(1 + edge_pressure) ** 1.5`. This directly tests whether stronger suppression of already-demanded links improves typical solutions while preserving demand-weighted paths and transit-node discounts.

4. **Cap or adapt the two-hop reward per OD pair.** Retain normalized OD demand as the basis for path rewards, but cap the total reward across all intermediates for each pair, or modestly reduce the reward for the highest-demand pairs. This tests whether limiting indirect reward prevents it from over-influencing topology selection while retaining the current successful direct-service priority.

5. **Tune the evaluator-supplied degree penalty near its current value of `1.0`**, testing a small range such as `0.8`, `1.1`, and `1.3` without changing the reward terms. The current program has strong gap performance but only 88–95% pre-repair feasibility across cases, so measuring feasibility and gap together could identify a better validity–quality trade-off.
