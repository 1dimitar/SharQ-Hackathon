# INDIVIDUAL PROGRAM SUMMARIES

The following are summaries of individual programs evaluated since the last meta update:

**Program Name: Demand-Weighted Linear Topology Surrogate**
- **Implementation**: Assigns each directed link a linear weight equal to negative demand normalized by the matrix maximum, with no quadratic terms and a degree-penalty weight of 1.0. The evaluator receives the terms and runtime through a fixed entry point.
- **Performance**: Combined score was 0.80; mean best gap was 12.76%, median gap 40.37%, feasibility 100%, and the model used zero quadratic terms.
- **Feedback**: All cases produced 64 distinct topologies; three had 100% valid samples before repair, while network10_s3 had 98%. Best gaps ranged from 10.7% to 15.9%, with network10_s3 worst, indicating the simple demand-only surrogate yields valid but suboptimal topologies.

**Program Name: Demand-Weighted Two-Hop Surrogate QUBO**
- **Implementation**: Assigns negative linear weights to directed links in proportion to demand, then adds negative quadratic rewards for two-hop paths, discounted by transit-node pressure and direct edge demand. Returns a degree-penalty weight of 1.0; construction is efficient for the tested 10-node networks.
- **Performance**: Combined score was 0.81, with a 12.30% mean best gap, 36.41% mean median gap, 0.99 feasible rate, and 480 quadratic terms.
- **Feedback**: The method produced topologies close to the reference best, but median gaps were substantially larger; the largest best gap was 15.6% on network10_s1. It generated 64 distinct topologies per case and built in under 0.01 seconds.

# GLOBAL INSIGHTS SCRATCHPAD

The following are global insights about optimization approaches and their effectiveness:

## Successful Algorithmic Patterns
- **The current best, Generation 64’s *Demand-Weighted Two-Hop QUBO Surrogate*, remains strongest on gap quality among the compared results:** score **0.83**, mean best gap **10.77%**, and mean median gap **31.61%**. It combines normalized direct-demand rewards with two-hop rewards proportional to through-demand, discounted by both transit-node pressure and pressure on the two component links.
- **Generation 70’s *Pressure-Discounted Two-Hop QUBO Surrogate* is the closest new comparison:** score **0.83**, with **10.17%** mean best gap and **34.13%** mean median gap. Its concave direct-demand signal and pressure-discounted paths improve best-sample quality over Generations 65–68, but its median gap is **2.52 percentage points worse** than Generation 64’s.
- **Pressure-discounted two-hop rewards consistently outperform the weaker new alternatives on best gaps.** Generation 68’s *Capped Two-Hop Demand-Weighted QUBO* scores **0.81** with a **12.34%** best gap; Generation 66’s *Pressure-Discounted Two-Hop QUBO Surrogate* scores **0.82** with **12.86%**; Generation 65 scores **0.81** with **14.19%**. Generation 70’s pressure-discounted design improves on all three in best gap.
- **Demand-targeted two-hop rewards remain effective without increasing model size.** Generation 64 and the new Generations 65–70 all use **480 quadratic terms**. The current best’s 480-term formulation achieves substantially better median gaps than the new variants, reinforcing that reward allocation and discounting matter more than simply adding terms.

## Ineffective Approaches
- **Endpoint activity and generic demand blends did not match the current best’s typical-sample quality.** Generation 65’s direct-demand/square-root/endpoint-volume blend scored **0.81** with **14.19%/36.25%** best/median gaps. Generation 67’s similar demand-and-endpoint formulation scored **0.80**, with **13.73%/36.97%** gaps.
- **Capping or balancing path rewards did not consistently improve median results.** Generation 68’s capped, transit-pressure-discounted path rewards achieved a strong **12.34%** best gap but a **38.47%** median gap—**6.86 points worse** than Generation 64’s. Its best case was `network10_s0` at a **9.1%** gap, but its largest was `s3` at **14.7%**.
- **High feasibility did not ensure stronger objective gaps.** Generations 65, 67, and 68 reported **98–100%** mean feasibility, compared with Generation 64’s **79%**, yet their mean best gaps were worse (**12.34–14.19%**) and their median gaps were markedly worse (**36.25–38.47%**). Generation 66 also had **96%** feasibility but gaps of **12.86%/34.36%**.
- **Generation 70’s concave direct signal appears to favor the best samples over the typical sample.** It has a slightly better best gap than Generation 64 (**10.17% versus 10.77%**) but a worse median gap (**34.13% versus 31.61%**); its case-level best gaps are consistently **9.5–11.3%**, while median gaps range from **31.5–37.0%**.

## Implementation Insights
- **Generation 64’s direct-link reward is deliberately simple:** `-demand[i,j] / max(demand)` for each off-diagonal pair. The previous comparisons found that adding endpoint-activity or capacity features did not improve median gaps; the new endpoint-blended Generations 65 and 67 likewise trail Generation 64.
- **Generation 64’s path term is both demand-supported and pressure-discounted.** It skips nonpositive through-demand and uses `path_strength = 0.56`, scaled by `demand[i,k] / scale`, then divided by transit-node pressure and `1 + 0.5 * edge_pressure`. This produces **480** targeted terms and is associated with a **31.61%** median gap, better than every new program in this batch.
- **The new variants confirm that changing path weighting alone is not enough to improve typical outcomes.** Generation 68 adds a cap and transit-pressure discount but has the batch’s worst median gap (**38.47%**); Generation 70 discounts both transit pressure and edge demand and improves the best gap, but its **34.13%** median still trails Generation 64. All these formulations build in under **0.01 seconds** and use 480 terms, so runtime and term count do not explain the gap differences.
- **Feasibility is partly delegated to the evaluator.** Generation 64 returns `penalty_weight = 1.0` and has **75–88%** pre-repair validity across cases. The newer programs’ higher feasibility (up to 100%) did not translate into better gaps, indicating that the current best’s stronger surrogate objective quality comes with greater reliance on repair.

## Performance Analysis
- **Against the new batch, Generation 64 has the best median gap and ties the highest reported score.** Its score of **0.83** matches Generation 70’s, while its **31.61%** median gap beats Generation 70 (**34.13%**), Generation 66 (**34.36%**), Generation 65 (**36.25%**), Generation 67 (**36.97%**), and Generation 68 (**38.47%**). Generation 70 has a modestly lower mean best gap (**10.17% versus 10.77%**).
- **Generation 64 also remains strong against the historical pressure-discounted result.** Generation 57 scored **0.83** with an **11.66%** best gap and **30.61%** median gap: Generation 64 improves best-sample quality but is **1.00 point worse** on the median. Generation 70 improves on both of Generation 57’s best-gap and score metrics only in best gap and score tie; its median remains higher.
- **Generation 64’s instance-level weakness is `network10_s1`.** Its best gap ranges from **6.9%** on `s0` to **15.0%** on `s1`, with `s2` at **9.3%** and `s3` at **11.9%**. Generation 70’s best gaps are more tightly grouped (**9.5–11.3%**) but its median results vary more widely (**31.5–37.0%**).
- **Across all evaluated programs, best-sample and median performance are distinct.** Generation 70 leads the new batch on mean best gap, but Generation 64 leads on median gap; high feasibility in Generations 65–68 also does not correlate with lower gaps. The evaluations therefore distinguish models that occasionally find strong topologies from those that produce better typical samples.

# META RECOMMENDATIONS

The following are actionable recommendations for the next program generations:

1. **Sweep the two-hop reward strength around the current `path_strength = 0.56`.** Test values such as `0.46`, `0.50`, `0.62`, and `0.68`, leaving direct rewards and pressure discounts unchanged. This isolates a key part of the best-performing design and could improve the weak `network10_s1` result without discarding its strong median performance.

2. **Calibrate transit-node pressure using alternative normalization and floors.** Compare mean-incident normalization with maximum-incident normalization, and test floors such as `0.65` and `0.85` against the current `0.75`. Pressure-discounted paths are a consistent strength, but tuning their calibration may improve robustness across instances.

3. **Tune the component-edge pressure discount independently.** Keep the demand-supported path reward and node-pressure discount fixed, but compare `1 + 0.75 * edge_pressure` and `(1 + edge_pressure) ** 1.5` with the current `1 + 0.5 * edge_pressure`. This tests whether stronger suppression of paths involving already-demand-heavy links improves typical-sample quality.

4. **Cap the total two-hop reward per origin–destination pair.** Preserve the existing reward for each supported path, but limit the sum across intermediate nodes so many indirect routes cannot collectively dominate the direct-demand signal. This targets reward balance while retaining the sparse, demand-targeted 480-term structure associated with the best median gap.

5. **Test a mild concave transform of through-demand in the path reward.** For example, replace `through_demand / scale` with its square root, or blend the linear and square-root signals while keeping the direct-link rewards unchanged. Generation 70’s concave direct signal improved best-sample quality, so applying a restrained version specifically to two-hop rewards could capture that benefit without replacing the current program’s stronger overall median behavior.
