# GLOBAL INSIGHTS SCRATCHPAD

The following are global insights about optimization approaches and their effectiveness:

## Successful Algorithmic Patterns
- **The current best, Generation 10 “endpoint_pressure_mix,” is the strongest evaluated result:** score **0.82**, mean best gap **11.01%**, and mean median gap **35.91%**. Its linear reward combines normalized direct demand at **90%** with endpoint activity at **10%**, while discounted two-hop rewards add a complementary routing signal.
- **Demand-gated two-hop rewards appear useful for improving best-sample quality, but results vary by formulation.** Generation 10’s two-hop reward is discounted by transit-node load and shared across up to four route options; its **11.01%** mean best gap is better than Generation 7’s related 480-term variant (**12.33%**, score **0.81**) and Generation 5’s capacity-aware variant (**12.07%**, score **0.82**). The earlier reported best-gap result for the two-hop approach was **12.30%**; Generation 10 improves on it, though its median gap remains high.
- **Direct-demand weighting remains a robust baseline signal.** Generation 6’s endpoint-load-balance variant scored **0.80** with a **14.40%** best gap, while the current best’s stronger direct-demand emphasis and discounted route rewards achieved a substantially lower best gap. Previous evaluations also found a linear demand-weighted baseline competitive at **0.80**.

## Ineffective Approaches
- **Endpoint and shared-endpoint penalties did not beat the current best.** Generation 6 added positive quadratic penalties for high-demand links sharing an endpoint and scored **0.80**, with a **14.40%** best gap and **38.22%** median gap. Generation 9’s similar-alternative penalties scored **0.80**, with a **14.07%** best gap and **39.06%** median gap. These results are weaker than Generation 10’s **0.82** score and **11.01%** best gap.
- **Weak or more lightly weighted two-hop rewards were not consistently better.** Generation 7’s transit-discounted 480-term model scored **0.81**, but its best gap was **12.33%** and median gap **36.98%**. The current best’s more substantial indirect fraction (**0.6**, then divided across four candidate routes) improved the mean best gap, but this is not evidence that stronger route rewards always help: Generation 5’s related approach had a **12.07%** gap and Generation 7 still had a **16.9%** worst case.
- **Typical sample quality remains a weakness across demand-derived surrogates.** Despite the current best’s **11.01%** mean best gap, its mean median gap is **35.91%**. Generation 6 and Generation 9 had even higher median gaps (**38.22%** and **39.06%**), consistent with the previous finding that good best samples do not imply that typical samples closely match the reference objective.

## Implementation Insights
- **The current best normalizes and mixes two demand signals.** It scales direct demand by the matrix maximum and endpoint activity by the maximum endpoint sum, then combines them at **0.9/0.1** in each directed-link coefficient. This bounded mix preserves direct-demand emphasis without letting endpoint throughput dominate.
- **Its route term is demand-gated and load-discounted.** For distinct \(i,j,k\) with positive `demand[i,k]`, the coefficient rewards the selected pair \(i\to j\), \(j\to k\), scaled by normalized OD demand and `2 * mean_load / (mean_load + node_load[j])`. Dividing by four shares each OD reward across possible routes and yields **480 quadratic terms** in the tested 10-node cases.
- **Fixed degree-penalty strength and inexpensive construction preserve feasibility and speed.** Generation 10 uses `penalty_weight = 1.0`, builds in **under 0.01 seconds**, and has **0.98** mean feasibility (95%, 100%, 100%, and 98% across the four cases). This is slightly below the 100% feasibility reported for Generations 5–9, so the current best’s score improvement comes with a small feasibility tradeoff.
- **More quadratic terms have not reliably improved outcomes.** Generation 9 used **714** terms and scored **0.80**; Generation 5 and the current best each used **480** terms and scored **0.82**. As prior results also showed, term count alone does not explain solution quality.

## Performance Analysis
- **Generation 10 is the overall leader among these summaries:** its **0.82** score ties Generation 5’s top score and its **11.01%** mean best gap is lower than all the other newly listed programs: Generation 5 **12.07%**, Generation 7 **12.33%**, Generation 9 **14.07%**, and Generation 6 **14.40%**.
- **The current best improves best-sample quality but not typical-sample quality relative to the previous best baseline.** Compared with the previous insight’s current-best figures (**0.81** score, **12.80%** best gap, **35.12%** median gap, 100% feasibility), Generation 10 improves the score and best gap, but its median gap rises to **35.91%** and feasibility falls to **0.98**.
- **Generation 10’s best gaps vary by instance.** Its gaps range from **8.7%** on `network10_s0` to **13.5%** on `network10_s1`; the other cases are **12.2%** (`s2`) and **9.6%** (`s3`). This is a better mean best gap than the other new variants, but the `s1` result is still a clear weak case.
- **All variants generate diverse solution sets quickly, so diversity and build time do not distinguish their quality.** The feedback reports **64 distinct topologies** for every case and construction under **0.01 seconds** for the current best and the other evaluated programs. Their score and gap differences therefore coincide more with surrogate-term choices than with runtime or topology count.

# META RECOMMENDATIONS

The following are actionable recommendations for the next program generations:

1. **Sweep the direct-demand/endpoint-pressure mix around the current 90/10 setting.** Test 95/5, 85/15, and 80/20 while keeping both signals normalized as they are now. The 90/10 mixture is part of the strongest evaluated program, so nearby adjustments are a low-risk way to test whether endpoint activity can improve the weaker `network10_s1` result without sacrificing direct-demand emphasis.

2. **Tune the two-hop reward coefficient while leaving its structure intact.** Compare `indirect_fraction` values such as 0.4, 0.5, and 0.8 against the current 0.6, retaining demand gating, transit-load discounting, and division by four. Two-hop rewards have helped best-sample quality, but their results vary, so a focused sweep can identify whether their contribution is currently too weak or too strong.

3. **Gate two-hop rewards by the plausibility of both links in the route.** Multiply the existing OD-demand reward by a bounded factor derived from `demand[i,j]` and `demand[j,k]`, such as the geometric mean of their normalized values. This preserves the successful demand-gated routing signal while testing whether it avoids rewarding routes whose component links have little direct-demand support.

4. **Try a mild concave transform of direct demand to reduce dominance by the largest entries.** Replace the normalized direct-demand term with `sqrt(demand[i,j] / scale)`, or blend that transform with the current linear term while retaining a strong direct-demand contribution. This tests whether spreading reward toward moderate-demand links can improve typical topology quality, an area where the current program’s median gap remains high.

5. **Make the transit-node discount less dependent on global average load.** Compare the current factor with a bounded alternative based on `node_load[j]` relative to the maximum node load, while keeping the discount monotonic so heavily loaded nodes remain less attractive as intermediates. The current load discount is part of the best-performing route signal; adjusting its normalization may improve routing choices on unevenly loaded instances without adding more quadratic terms.
