# GLOBAL INSIGHTS SCRATCHPAD

The following are global insights about optimization approaches and their effectiveness:

## Successful Algorithmic Patterns
- **The current best remains Generation 5, `sparse_relay_support`, with a combined score of 0.85, an 11.36% mean best gap, and a 25.86% mean median gap.** Its strongest pattern is pairing direct-link rewards based on `0.75*value + 0.25*sqrt(value)` with a bounded set of pressure-discounted, balanced-affinity relay candidates. It reaches that result with 264 quadratic terms.
- **Sparse, pressure-aware relay selection continues to outperform broad relay support.** Generation 5’s 264-term design scores 0.85, while Generation 21’s 880-term pressure-discounted two-hop model scores 0.81 and Generation 24’s 966-term congestion-oriented model scores 0.77. Generation 23 also uses just 264 terms and scores 0.85, but its 10.94% best gap and 26.69% median gap are slightly worse than Generation 5’s corresponding gaps.
- **Blending linear and square-root demand signals is a recurring useful component, but the relay ranking and reward construction matter.** Generation 22’s similar direct-link blend and weaker pressure-discounted relay rewards score 0.85 with a 10.20% best gap, yet have a 27.66% median gap. Generation 5’s more specific balanced-leg affinity ranking produces the better median gap among these tied-score programs.
- **Best-sample performance and typical-sample performance remain distinct.** Generation 5’s best gaps are +10.0% on `network12_s0` and +12.8% on `network12_s1`, but median gaps are +24.0% and +27.7%. Its result is strong on best-found quality, not uniformly strong across sampled solutions.

## Ineffective Approaches
- **Broad two-hop support and larger QUBOs did not improve results.** Generation 21 uses 880 terms and scores 0.81; Generation 24 uses 966 terms and scores 0.77. Both have sizable median gaps and low pre-repair feasibility (20% and 2%, respectively), indicating that additional interactions did not reliably steer QAOA toward better valid topologies.
- **Route-diversity or extra affinity rewards did not beat the compact relay design.** Generation 17’s 1,496-term route-diversity model scores 0.76, with a 21.49% mean best gap, 42.47% median gap, and 24% feasibility. The higher feasibility than some alternatives did not translate into stronger objectives.
- **Sparse support alone is insufficient when reward calibration or relay selection changes.** Generation 23’s bilateral relay-affinity model matches Generation 5’s 0.85 score but has slightly worse best and median gaps and only 14% mean feasibility. Earlier sparse variants also trailed: Generation 20 scored 0.83 with 188 terms, while Generation 19 scored 0.83 with 176 terms. The outcomes favor the particular ranking-and-reward combination, not sparsity by itself.
- **Feasibility is not a reliable proxy for objective quality.** Generation 17 has 24% feasibility but a 0.76 score, while Generation 22 scores 0.85 despite only 8% feasibility. Generation 5 likewise scores 0.85 with 8% feasibility, so its strong best results coexist with substantial reliance on evaluator repair.

## Implementation Insights
- **Generation 5’s concrete relay budget controls model size:** for each positive-demand OD pair, it selects `min(n-2, max(2, n//4))` relays. This yields 264 quadratic terms on network12, versus 880 in Generation 21 and 966 in Generation 24, without sacrificing best-score performance.
- **Its relay ranking combines balanced leg affinity with transit pressure.** The program ranks relays using `sqrt((0.1+a)*(0.1+b)) / (0.75 + node_pressure)`, where pressure is based on the larger normalized incoming or outgoing demand and has a 0.65 floor. This is more targeted than simply expanding relay support; Generation 5’s results support the value of selecting a limited set using both path plausibility and node load.
- **Consistent preprocessing and canonicalized interactions make the construction well-defined.** Generation 5 clips negative traffic, clears the diagonal, normalizes by maximum demand, applies the same linear/square-root blend to direct rewards and path support, and sorts the two edge keys before inserting each quadratic term. These choices accompany a compact model with strong best-found results.
- **The new evaluations reinforce that penalty weight alone does not explain performance.** Generation 5 uses weight 1.5 and scores 0.85, matching Generation 23’s fixed weight of 1.5 but slightly outperforming it on gaps. Generation 22 uses weight 6.0 and also scores 0.85, while Generation 21 leaves the penalty to the evaluator and scores 0.81. The relay terms and their calibration vary alongside these weights, so the results do not show a simple weight-to-quality relationship.

## Performance Analysis
- **Generation 5 remains the overall leader among the reported programs.** Its 0.85 combined score and 11.36% mean best gap remain stronger overall than Generation 17 (0.76; 21.49%), Generation 21 (0.81; 13.68%), and Generation 24 (0.77; 21.55%). Its median gap of 25.86% is also below those programs’ 42.47%, 34.17%, and 41.64%.
- **The 0.85-score alternatives offer different tradeoffs, not a clear replacement.** Generation 22 has a better mean best gap (10.20%) than Generation 5 but a worse median gap (27.66%) and equal 8% feasibility. Generation 23 has 10.94% best and 26.69% median gaps, with 14% feasibility. Generation 5’s balanced combination of score, best gap, and median gap keeps it the strongest overall result.
- **The current best’s two cases show uneven sampling outcomes.** `network12_s0` has a +10.0% best gap, +24.0% median gap, 10% raw feasibility, and 93 distinct topologies; `network12_s1` worsens to +12.8%, +27.7%, and 6% feasibility, with 62 distinct topologies. Both cases use 264 terms and 6,012 circuits, so the observed difference is case-specific rather than a change in model size.
- **Term count and feasibility do not predict score on their own.** Generation 17’s 1,496 terms and 24% feasibility accompany a 0.76 score; Generation 24’s 966 terms and 2% feasibility accompany 0.77; and Generation 5’s 264 terms and 8% feasibility accompany 0.85. The comparisons support evaluating term selection and reward quality alongside size and raw validity.

# META RECOMMENDATIONS

The following are actionable recommendations for the next program generations:

1. **Sweep the relay budget while preserving the current ranking and weights.** Compare `min(n-2, max(1, n//5))`, the current `min(n-2, max(2, n//4))`, and `min(n-2, n//3)` candidates per positive-demand OD pair. The compact 264-term design already beats broader relay models, so test small, controlled changes rather than expanding support indiscriminately.

2. **Give additional relay candidates only to the highest-demand OD pairs.** Keep the current budget for most pairs, but add one candidate for pairs in the top demand quantile, subject to a strict total-term cap. This tests whether targeted support can help the weaker `network12_s1` result without the broad two-hop interactions that underperformed.

3. **Test a more bottleneck-sensitive relay ranking.** Replace the geometric-mean affinity `sqrt((0.1+a)*(0.1+b))` with a score that penalizes the weaker leg more strongly, such as `sqrt((0.1+min(a,b))*(0.1+max(a,b)))` or `min(a,b) + 0.25*max(a,b)`. Keep the pressure discount, relay budget, and reward weights fixed to isolate whether more conservative path selection improves typical solution quality.

4. **Tune the pressure discount consistently in both relay ranking and reward.** Replace `(0.75 + node_pressure)` with `(0.75 + node_pressure)**alpha` for a small sweep around `alpha = 1` (for example, 0.75 and 1.25). Pressure-aware relay selection is part of the strongest design; this tests whether it should more or less strongly avoid loaded transit nodes without changing the sparse structure.

5. **Tune direct and relay reward strengths independently.** First vary the direct-link blend around `0.75*value + 0.25*sqrt(value)`, leaving relay rewards unchanged; in separate variants, vary the quadratic multiplier around `0.25` while preserving the same support and ranking. This builds on a demand blend that has performed well while avoiding simultaneous changes to the reward calibration and relay selection.
