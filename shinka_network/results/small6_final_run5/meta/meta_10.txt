# GLOBAL INSIGHTS SCRATCHPAD

The following are global insights about optimization approaches and their effectiveness:

## Successful Algorithmic Patterns
- **The current best, Demand-Aware Sparse QUBO Surrogate (Generation 8), ties the top combined score at 0.90** and improves on the earlier Generation 4 result’s 4.04% mean post-processing gap, reaching **4.12%**—close to that benchmark while retaining a sparse, demand-aware route model. It combines direct-link rewards blending normalized demand and its square root with pressure-ranked two-hop rewards and reciprocal-link penalties.
- **Sparse, pressure-aware two-hop selection remains a strong pattern across generations.** Generation 9, Pressure-Aware Sparse Route QUBO, scored 0.89 with 383 quadratic terms and a 4.86% post-processing gap; Generation 10, Sparse Demand-Aware Surrogate QUBO, also scored 0.89 with 1,011.83 terms and a 4.65% gap. Generation 8 achieves 0.90 with only **467.17 terms**, indicating that this balance can deliver strong repaired quality without the denser Generation 10 model.
- **Demand-sensitive route support and direct/indirect reward balance are consistently effective.** Generation 8 ranks intermediates by edge affinity discounted by node pressure, selects two or four routes depending on demand quantile, and adds an alternative route. This continues the successful demand-aware sparse-routing pattern identified in Generation 4, whose 0.90 score and 4.04% post gap remain the strongest reported post-processing result.
- **The post-processor benefits from models that provide a useful topology head start.** Generation 8’s post score is 0.96, with a 4.12% mean post gap; its instance gaps range from **0.94% (network12_s1)** to **7.35% (network13_s1)**. This substantial repair improvement is consistent with earlier programs, but the current model’s head-start score of 0.961 is particularly strong.

## Ineffective Approaches
- **Raw sampled topology quality remains the main weakness, even in the best-scoring program.** Generation 8 has a **10.83% mean best-sample gap** and **28.72% median gap**, despite its 0.90 combined score. Its largest best-sample gap is **14.5% on network15_s0**, while the post-processor reduces that instance to 5.11%; the repaired score therefore masks considerably weaker unprocessed samples.
- **More quadratic terms do not reliably improve scores.** Generation 10 uses about **1,012 terms** yet scores 0.89, below Generation 8’s 0.90 with 467 terms. Generation 7 also scores 0.89 with 1,061 terms. These results do not support term count alone as a route-quality proxy.
- **Pressure-aware route rewards without sufficiently strong pre-repair topology quality leave large median gaps.** Generation 5 and Generation 9 report median gaps of 35.81% and 32.14%, respectively; Generation 8 improves to 28.72%, but still has a large gap between its best samples and typical samples. Across these programs, the fixed post-processor is doing substantial work rather than merely refining already strong typical samples.
- **A fixed or modest constraint penalty does not ensure uniformly feasible samples.** Generation 8’s size-adjusted penalty still yields a mean feasibility of 0.96, with individual validity rates as low as **91% on network15_s2**. Earlier fixed-penalty models also show feasibility variation, so penalty weighting alone does not eliminate invalid samples.

## Implementation Insights
- **Generation 8 sanitizes demand before constructing terms:** it clips negative values to zero, clears the diagonal, scales by maximum traffic, and uses both normalized demand and its square root. This produces stable direct rewards and avoids self-link contributions; the resulting model reaches 0.90 combined score with 96% mean feasibility.
- **Its route ranking explicitly accounts for both edge evidence and transit pressure.** For each positive-demand pair, it scores intermediates using `0.25 + normalized[i,j] + normalized[j,k]`, divided by pressure raised to 0.75. It keeps up to four candidates for demand at or above the 75th percentile and two otherwise, then retains a pressure-discounted alternative. This is a concrete implementation of sparse, demand-dependent route coverage.
- **Reciprocal-link terms distinguish useful mutual demand from wasteful backtracking.** The program adds a positive coupling proportional to mean direct reward and network depth, then discounts that penalty when both directions have demand using `1 - 0.45 * mutual`. This reflects the Generation 8 summary’s reciprocal-link penalty pattern and is compatible with strong feasibility and post-processing results.
- **The implementation is computationally lightweight and scales predictably across evaluated sizes.** Construction is typically reported as 0.00 seconds (one 0.07-second case); quadratic terms rise from **257 at n=10** to **654 at n=16**. This is substantially sparser than Generation 10’s roughly 1,012 terms while retaining comparable post-processed quality.

## Performance Analysis
- **Generation 8 is tied for the best combined score at 0.90**, matching Generation 4’s reported 0.90. Its score components are **0.84 raw and 0.96 post**, with a 4.12% post gap. Generation 4 has a slightly better reported post gap of 4.04%, while Generation 8’s raw score of 0.84 is a strong result among the reported programs.
- **Against the other recent programs, Generation 8 has a favorable quality–size balance.** Generation 7 scores 0.89 with 1,061 terms and a 4.41% post gap; Generation 10 scores 0.89 with 1,011.83 terms and a 4.65% gap; Generation 9 scores 0.89 with 383 terms and a 4.86% gap. Generation 8 combines a higher score with fewer terms than Generations 7 and 10, though Generation 9 is sparser.
- **The post-processor consistently narrows large raw gaps.** Generation 8’s mean best gap of 10.83% falls to 4.12%; examples include network12_s1 improving from 12.0% to 0.94% and network10_s0 from 8.8% to 1.31%. The mean median gap of 28.72%, however, confirms that typical sampled solutions remain much weaker than the repaired results.
- **Performance varies by network size and instance rather than degrading monotonically.** Generation 8’s sample-quality component ranges from 0.828 at n=12 to 0.850 at n=10, while head-start quality ranges from 0.942 at n=13 to 0.979 at n=10. Post gaps are especially low on network12_s1 (0.94%) and network16_s1 (1.69%), but reach 7.35% on network13_s1; network15_s0 has the worst raw gap at 14.5%.

# META RECOMMENDATIONS

The following are actionable recommendations for the next program generations:

1. **Choose route counts using demand and candidate-score margins.** Preserve the current four-route allowance for high-demand pairs and two-route allowance for others, but retain additional intermediates when the top affinity-to-pressure ranks are close; reduce alternatives when one candidate clearly dominates. This extends the sparse, demand-aware routing pattern while directing terms toward genuinely ambiguous pairs instead of increasing model density uniformly.

2. **Discount intermediates by predicted route load, not just aggregate incident traffic.** After ranking candidate routes, estimate each node’s transit load from the demand of pairs whose selected paths use it, then apply a modest additional pressure discount to candidates with high predicted load. The current pressure-aware ranking already performs well; this route-specific refinement could reduce congestion-prone choices without abandoning its sparse two-hop structure.

3. **Make indirect rewards and direct-to-leg support depend on endpoint affinity.** For each candidate path \(i \to j \to k\), scale its reward using the normalized strengths of both legs and their relationship to direct demand \(i \to k\), rather than relying only on direct support and candidate rank. Keep the existing pressure discount and test a small number of weight settings, so well-evidenced alternatives gain support without letting weak legs inflate the QUBO.

4. **Add a tightly capped set of three-hop alternatives only for difficult pairs.** Retain the existing two-hop routes as the foundation, and add a three-hop route when a pair has weak or closely tied two-hop candidates, or exceptionally high demand. Rank these paths by endpoint-leg affinity and pressure, and cap additions per pair to test whether they improve difficult cases such as network13_s1 without the cost and dilution of exhaustive path enumeration.

5. **Target feasibility penalties at highly coupled variables.** The current size-adjusted penalty yields 96% mean feasibility but falls to 91% on network15_s2; modestly increase penalties for links participating in many route-reward terms, while leaving less-connected variables near the current penalty. Compare feasibility and raw sample quality together, since the substantial raw-to-post gap suggests stronger constraints may help, but excessive penalties could suppress the useful demand and route rewards.
