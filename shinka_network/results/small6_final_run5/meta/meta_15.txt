# GLOBAL INSIGHTS SCRATCHPAD

The following are global insights about optimization approaches and their effectiveness:

## Successful Algorithmic Patterns
- **The current best, Generation 8’s Demand-Aware Sparse QUBO Surrogate, remains a strong quality–size balance:** it scores **0.90** (raw **0.84**, post **0.96**) with a **4.12%** mean post-processed gap and about **467 quadratic terms**. Its blend of normalized-demand and square-root direct rewards, pressure-discounted two-hop routes, and reciprocal-link penalties is consistent with the prior successful sparse-routing pattern.
- **Sparse, pressure-aware two-hop rewards perform well across several designs.** Generation 14’s Transit-Aware Sparse QUBO also scores **0.90**, with a slightly better **4.49%** post gap and only **184–467 terms**; it estimates transit loads via damped soft-routing updates before selecting a small set of intermediates. Generation 11’s affinity-weighted routes likewise scores **0.90**, with a **3.98%** post-processed gap.
- **Demand-aware direct rewards combined with route support are a recurring strong pattern.** Generation 13’s margin-congestion model scores **0.89**, with **99% feasibility** and a **4.20%** post gap, using normalized and square-root demand rewards plus sparse two-hop and shared-node congestion terms. Generation 12’s size-scaled degree-penalty model also scores **0.88**, with a **4.46%** post gap.
- **Repair benefits from a useful but not necessarily near-optimal sampled topology.** The current best’s mean best-sample gap of **10.83%** falls to **4.12%** after repair. Generation 11 reports an even larger drop—from **12.10%** best-sample gap to **3.98%** post-processed gap—showing that strong repaired results are shared across these demand-aware models.

## Ineffective Approaches
- **Typical raw samples remain weak, even when the repaired score is high.** Generation 8’s median gap is **28.72%**, far above its **10.83%** best-sample gap; Generation 14’s median gap is **30.22%**, and Generation 12’s is **35.81%**. Thus, these models’ high combined scores do not reflect consistently strong unprocessed samples.
- **More elaborate or dense models have not shown a reliable score advantage.** The previous comparisons found Generation 10 at **0.89** with roughly **1,012 terms**, below Generation 8’s **0.90** with about **467 terms**. Generation 13 generates **906–3,881 terms** yet scores **0.89**, while Generation 14 reaches **0.90** with only **184–467**. Term count alone is not a useful predictor of quality.
- **Feasibility and raw quality still vary by instance and size.** Generation 8 averages **96% feasibility**, but individual cases fall to **91%** on network15_s2; its worst raw gap is **14.5%** on network15_s0. Generation 14 averages **95% feasibility** and has a **15.3%** worst best-sample gap on network16_s1. These results show that demand-aware rewards and modest penalties do not guarantee uniformly strong samples.
- **The demand-trained quadratic approach is less robust at larger sizes.** Generation 6 achieves **0.86** and 100% feasibility, but its largest reported best-solution gap is **21.2%** on network16_s0. Its trained surrogate and elite-derived biases therefore did not match the stronger overall scores of the sparse route-reward models.

## Implementation Insights
- **Generation 8’s demand sanitization and scaling are practical foundations for stable terms.** It clips negative demand, clears the diagonal, scales by maximum traffic, and blends normalized demand with its square root. It then uses the same demand support in two-hop rewards, avoiding self-link contributions and keeping rewards comparable across instances.
- **Its route selection combines evidence and transit pressure rather than relying on demand alone.** For each positive-demand pair, it ranks intermediates by edge affinity discounted by node pressure to the power of **0.75**, keeps up to four candidates for higher-demand pairs and two otherwise, then adds a pressure-favored alternative. The resulting model scores **0.90** while using about **467 terms**.
- **The reciprocal-link coupling is a targeted structural penalty.** Generation 8 penalizes links that create unproductive two-hop backtracking, but discounts that penalty when demand is substantial in both directions. This helps preserve useful reciprocal links while discouraging wasteful expansions; it coexists with **96% mean feasibility** and a **4.12% post gap**.
- **Other strong implementations use different mechanisms to estimate route value without making the model dense.** Generation 14 performs three damped soft-routing updates to estimate transit load and produces **184–467 terms**; Generation 11 uses affinity-weighted sparse routes and reaches a **3.98% post gap**. Generation 13 adds shared-node congestion couplings but produces substantially more terms, without exceeding the current best’s combined score.

## Performance Analysis
- **Generation 8 remains tied at the top combined score with the other reported 0.90 programs.** Its score is **0.9003** (sample-quality **0.8398**, head-start **0.9607**), while Generation 11 and Generation 14 are also reported at **0.90**. Generation 11 has the lowest stated post gap among these newer summaries (**3.98%**), compared with Generation 8’s **4.12%** and Generation 14’s **4.49%**.
- **Generation 8 retains a favorable balance of raw sample quality and repair head start.** Its mean best-sample gap is **10.83%**, and post-processing reduces this to **4.12%**. Generation 11 has a slightly worse best gap (**12.10%**) but a lower post gap (**3.98%**); Generation 14 reports an **11.36%** best gap and **4.49%** post gap. The available metrics show close competition, not a single model leading on every measure.
- **Generation 8’s instance results expose where its aggregate score is strongest and weakest.** Post gaps range from **0.94%** on network12_s1 to **7.35%** on network13_s1; raw best gaps peak at **14.5%** on network15_s0. Despite that worst raw case, its mean head-start component is **0.9607**, and the repair substantially reduces the average gap.
- **The broader results reinforce that sparse routing and repair quality matter more than construction complexity alone.** Generation 12, 13, and 14 report post gaps of **4.46%**, **4.20%**, and **4.49%**, respectively, around the same range as Generation 8’s **4.12%**. Generation 13’s much larger term counts do not translate into a higher combined score, while the sparse designs reach **0.90** with hundreds or fewer terms.

# META RECOMMENDATIONS

The following are actionable recommendations for the next program generations:

1. **Adapt the number of retained two-hop routes to candidate-score margins.** Keep the current four-route allowance for high-demand pairs and two-route allowance for others, but retain more candidates when their affinity-to-pressure ranks are close and fewer when one candidate clearly dominates. This focuses the existing sparse model’s terms on ambiguous routes rather than increasing density uniformly.

2. **Refine pressure discounts using estimated transit load.** After choosing candidate routes, estimate each intermediate’s transit load from the demand of pairs whose routes use it, then apply a modest additional discount to routes through heavily loaded nodes. This extends the successful pressure-aware two-hop pattern with route-specific congestion information, while keeping the route set sparse.

3. **Scale indirect rewards by evidence from both legs.** For each \(i \to j \to k\) candidate, incorporate the normalized strengths of \(i \to j\) and \(j \to k\), and compare their support with direct demand \(i \to k\), instead of relying mainly on candidate rank and endpoint demand. Keep the existing pressure discount and test modest weight changes so weak legs do not gain excessive reward.

4. **Add a small, targeted set of three-hop paths for difficult pairs.** Preserve two-hop routes as the foundation, and consider a three-hop alternative only for exceptionally high-demand pairs or pairs with weak or closely tied two-hop candidates. Rank additions by leg affinity and intermediate pressure, with a strict per-pair cap to test whether longer routes help difficult cases without making the QUBO dense.

5. **Localize constraint penalties to highly coupled links.** Identify links that participate in many route-reward terms and modestly increase their constraint penalties, while leaving less-connected links near the current size-scaled penalty. This targets the observed feasibility dips—especially the 91% rate on network15_s2—without uniformly strengthening penalties and potentially suppressing useful demand rewards.
